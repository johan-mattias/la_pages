When students improve, when they stall, and when they drop

Seven analyses of one introductory programming course, read as a single story through Bandura’s social cognitive theory. Improvement here means growing self-efficacy: a student’s belief that they can program. Its strongest source is mastery experience, succeeding at something that took effort, and the course logs record those experiences one run, one exercise and one week at a time.

This page follows them through the school year: where they pile up, where practice goes on without producing them, and where they stop. It adds no new analysis; every number is taken from the chapter it links to.

1 · The yardstick

Improvement means self-efficacy, and self-efficacy is built from mastery

Self-efficacy is a person’s belief that they can do what a task demands. For a programming student, that means believing they can write a program, trace what it does and get it working again when it breaks. Social cognitive theory names four sources of that belief: mastery experience, seeing people like you succeed, encouragement from others, and how your body and mood respond. Mastery experience is the strongest. Success builds the belief and failure undermines it, most of all before the belief is established, and the sturdiest self-efficacy comes from obstacles overcome through effort rather than from easy wins.

The belief then feeds back into behaviour. It shapes what students choose to attempt, how much effort they spend and how long they keep going when stuck, and so how many new mastery experiences they get. That loop is the mechanism the whole story rests on, and every chapter observes some part of it.

↗Improving

The loop closes. Attempts end in errors fixed and exercises finished, and the student keeps coming back for more.

→Stalling

The loop spins without closing. The student keeps running code, but errors repeat, debugging turns into trial and error, and sessions stretch out.

↘Dropping

The loop slows, stops or turns against the student. Weeks are missed, work goes missing, or failures pile up faster than they are overcome.

One survey wave sets the limits. No page can show a student’s self-efficacy rising, because it was measured once. What the pages can show is the trail of mastery experiences the end-of-course belief is built from, and the grades it tracks (ρ .60). The story reads improvement through those sources and treats the survey as where the trail leads, so its claims are associations, not effects.

2 · The answer in brief

Improves, stalls, drops

Read across the seven chapters, the findings sort into three groups. Each card names a condition, gives the strongest number behind it and links to the evidence.

↗

Students improve when…

…they finish what they start.

The share of attempted exercises a student completes is the strongest log correlate of self-efficacy (ρ .41). The pass rate of graded submissions is unrelated (ρ .04). Finishing counts, however many tries it takes.

Ch 2What goes with high self-efficacyCh 3Behaviour and self-efficacy

…the error message names the fix.

Fix times fall with every encounter for a missing colon (from 16 s at the first encounter to 6 s after 33 or more), an unclosed bracket, = for == and a missing indented block. 85% of students speed up on syntax errors, and the gain holds on the first such error of each new day.

Ch 5Escape testCh 5Individual students

…they come back often, and beyond the lesson.

Short gaps between active days go with higher self-efficacy (ρ −.38 for the median gap). Students who do much of their work in the evening and at weekends report the highest self-efficacy of four working styles, and in te22a after-school minutes track the final-project grade (ρ .64).

…the final project offers a second chance.

In TE23, 26 of 63 students ended above their February estimate, in step with the project grade (ρ .73), and three of the four two-step climbers got an A on it. The least active trajectory partly recovers for the project too.

…low starters do the work.

Climbers began below the median on the entry test, but handed everything in, worked outside class as much as the strongest group, and ended with self-efficacy close to theirs (5.2 against 5.6 of 7).

→

Practice stalls when…

…the error is about meaning, not form.

NameError, AttributeError and type errors do not get faster with practice; only 14% of students speed up on runtime errors. One run in five ends in an error in every unit, because each new construct brings new errors.

Ch 5Escape testCh 5Errors across units

…debugging turns into trial and error.

30% of debugging episodes pass through two or more different errors, and each extra error adds about a minute. Experience does not reduce it; loops, strings and lists raise it.

Ch 5Whack-a-mole

…students rerun instead of revising.

After a compile error the next action is another run 60% of the time and an edit 30%: unchanged code, run again 21,000 times. After a failed submission it is a rerun 69% of the time and an edit 20%.

Ch 3The edit–run–error loop

…sessions stretch out.

Long sessions go with lower self-efficacy (ρ −.28), consistent with struggle rather than investment. The working style with the longest in-school sessions reports the lowest self-efficacy of the four.

Ch 3Behaviour and self-efficacyCh 2Long, dense sessions

…engagement settles into a lower gear.

119 of 159 students start in the most active state; by week 20, 23 are there and 92 are Low. They are not dropping out; they are practising less, which leaves less room for new mastery. In te22a, 16 students were fully active per lesson in the spring against 20 in the autumn.

Ch 3Weekly statesCh 6In the lessons

…and the survey says the same.

Persistence and debugging is the lowest core self-efficacy subscale in every class. Students are confident tracing code and writing conditionals, much less so about getting unstuck.

Ch 1Self-efficacy by class
↘

Progress drops when…

…a week is missed.

Absence is sticky. After an absent week, 41% of students are absent again and 9% return to the most active state; after a most-active week, only 6% go absent. The case for acting on the first missed week.

Ch 3Weekly states

…the least active fall behind after unit 3.

In 13 of 14 ways of defining activity states, the lowest trajectory drops further after the first three units. In the default definition, a Low unit is followed by another Low unit 57% of the time.

Ch 4FindingsCh 4Trajectory explorer

…the course hits its wall.

The lowest exercise pass rates sit in units 8 and 9 (Flying letters: 4.2% of 665 attempts), and most students never pass anything in unit 10. The self-efficacy items rated lowest are the advanced topics, and students who never got that far left them blank.

Ch 3Where the course gets hard

…work goes missing.

In TE22, students with two or more missing unit or project grades averaged a final grade near E. Slippers started above the median but had the most missing work, and ended with self-efficacy as low as the struggling group (4.1 of 7).

Ch 1Missing workCh 1Four profiles

…a break interrupts practice.

School holidays empty whole weeks, and the first fixes after a long gap are slower: the missing colon takes 4.5% longer per doubling of the days since it was last met.

Ch 5Retention and recoveryCh 3The course calendar

…the class moves on without them.

In the US classes of chapter 7, roughly a quarter of students had not finished 80% of a unit’s lessons when their class moved on to the next. Classes that met more often kept about 7 percentage points more students up per doubling of meetings.

The smallest mastery experience: an error met and escaped. 5 · Open threads

What the story cannot say yet

Where the chapters disagree, where the theory asks for data the course does not record, and what would close the gaps.

  1. Whether self-efficacy actually rose. With one survey wave, improvement is read from its sources rather than observed. A second wave for the same students (the TE23 students who registered again as “te4” in August 2026) would turn the story’s inferences into measured change, and the climbers and slippers are the first students to check.

    Ch 1Self-efficacy and the SCT survey

  2. Finishing, or succeeding first time? The atlas found the pass rate of graded submissions unrelated to self-efficacy and proposed controlling for exercise difficulty with the catalogue’s points. The granularity study’s strongest correlate is close to that test: the points-weighted share of attempted exercises completed (ρ .41). Together they suggest that what goes with self-efficacy is eventually succeeding rather than succeeding first time, which fits Bandura’s point that success won through effort builds the sturdiest belief. A model with both measures and exercise difficulty would test it directly.

    Ch 3Behaviour and self-efficacyCh 2Strongest features

  3. Do the error profiles differ in self-efficacy? Chapter 5’s five error-solving profiles, from fluent fixers to often stuck, have not been linked to the survey. If the story holds, the often-stuck and whack-a-mole profiles should report the lowest persistence self-efficacy.

    Ch 5Five error-solving profilesCh 1Persistence subscale

  4. Bursty or regular? High self-efficacy goes with short gaps between active days in chapter 2 and with high day-to-day variability in chapter 3. Both can hold if confident students come back often and sometimes work in long bursts outside lessons, but one model with both measures should check it.

    Ch 2Active-day regularityCh 3Day-to-day variability

  5. The sources the logs cannot see. Seeing peers succeed and being encouraged leave no trace in the platform. The help-seeking items are the nearest proxy: climbers asked others for help the most, slippers the least.

    Ch 1Goals, help-seeking and habits

  6. Who is missing. 57 logged students, 36% of the total, have no class label or survey response, so they are invisible to every behaviour-to-self-efficacy result. Their activity looks like everyone else’s.

    Ch 3The files

  7. Student or setting? Class membership shapes the trajectories (2 of 27 te22a students are in the most active trajectory, against 21 of 32 in te22b), and the timetable sets how much practice fits in a week. Standardising within class and modelling the timetable would separate the student from the setting.
