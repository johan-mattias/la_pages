How often and how long should programming classes meet?

We rebuilt the timetables of nearly two thousand US classes from one year of CS1 activity logs. Between two and five meetings a week, how often a class met made no difference to the class time a unit took; weekly progress simply followed weekly class time. Sessions over an hour covered more per meeting but less per hour.

What classes do

Two thirds of clean classes met every school day, about 50 minutes; 17% used 85-minute blocks on alternating days. Classes meeting once a week or less were almost absent.

Meeting frequency

Class hours per unit did not differ between 2 and 5 meetings a week. Fewer meetings meant more weeks per unit: +32% at four days, +51% on alternating days.

Session length

Compared with 45–59 minutes, a unit took 13% more class hours with 60–74-minute sessions and 21% more with sessions of 90 minutes or longer.

Teachers set the pace

Within the same teacher, units took about the same number of weeks whatever the timetable. More frequent meetings went with slightly more students keeping up.

1 · Introduction

A timetable is decided before the teacher meets the class

One school teaches programming in a daily 50-minute period, another in 90-minute blocks every other day, a third once a week. Research on distributed practice suggests that spreading study over more sessions helps retention, while every session also costs time to log in and get back into a program, and long sessions leave room for extended work. Little evidence exists for programming classes specifically.

Online curricula used in classrooms log when a class works, for how long, and how far each student gets. The logs do not say which students form a class or when it meets, so we inferred classes, sessions and timetables from students' activity, and asked three questions:

  • RQ1What meeting frequencies and session lengths do classes using an online introductory programming curriculum follow?
  • RQ2How is meeting frequency associated with progress through the curriculum, per session, per class hour and per school week, and with how many students keep up?
  • RQ3How is session length associated with the same measures?
2 · Data and method

From 345 million clicks to 734 measurable classes

The data cover the CS1 course of CMU CS Academy, a free online curriculum that teaches Python through interactive graphics in 12 units. Each unit has sections that open with notes, interactive reading pages with short checkpoint questions, followed by autograded exercises, and ends with practice, creative and collaborative tasks and short assessments.

The export contains every student event from 1 July 2024 to 30 June 2025, a table linking each student to a teacher with the school's state and country, and the course's exercise list. It contains no class identifiers, rosters or timetables. Timestamps were converted to local time with the time zone of the school's state.

The data were provided by CMU CS Academy and are approved for research use. Students and teachers appear only as numeric identifiers and schools only by state; results are reported as aggregates over classes.

  1. Presence

    Each student is marked present in every 5-minute bin with any event.

  2. Classes

    A teacher's students who are active in the same bins on the same days are clustered into classes.

  3. Sessions

    A session is a window in which a share of the class works together, plus low-use days in the class's usual period.

  4. Timetables

    Weekdays with a session in at least half of the available weeks are the class's scheduled days.

  5. Lesson progress

    For each unit and section: when half the regular students started it and when half had finished it.

  6. Quality filter

    Only classes passing ten rules on size, regularity and data quality are compared.
Lesson and "finished"

A lesson is one of the 55 notes pages with checkpoint questions (150 questions in total). A student has finished a lesson once every question on it was answered correctly. Students finished 94% of the lessons they opened.

Timetable categories

1–5 days a week by the number of scheduled weekdays, if at least 80% of session days fall on them; "alternating" for every-other-day block schedules (all weekday rates between 0.3 and 0.7, mostly two-day gaps); "every other week" and "monthly" for fixed weekdays met less than weekly; everything else is irregular and left out. Holidays and the teacher's breaks are not counted as available days.

Session length

The median length of a class's sessions on its scheduled days: the time the class worked on the platform together, which can be shorter than the school period.

Pace

A class starts a unit when half of its regular students have opened one of its lessons, and finishes it when half of the regular students present at the start have finished 80% of its lessons. Pace is the sessions, class hours and school weeks between the two; lessons per session (hour, week) is the same pace on a readable scale. It covers the lessons and the exercises between them, not the practice, creative tasks and quizzes after a unit's last lesson.

Keeping up

The share of a class's students who had finished at least 80% of a unit's lessons when the class started the next unit.

Validation of the inferred classes

On California (2,706 students, 93 teachers), an independently designed detector agreed on 99.4% of student pairs that both placed, and matched sessions started a median of 1 minute and ended 2 minutes apart. The second detector placed fewer students (1,816 against 2,184).

Statistics

Means by group with 95% intervals from resampling teachers (1,000 draws). Regressions at class-unit level with unit and time-zone fixed effects, standard errors clustered by teacher; a second set compares only classes of the same teacher.

3 · Results

What the timetables show

RQ1 · Timetables and session lengths

Two thirds of the 871 clean classes met five days a week. Alternating-day block schedules were common in the Eastern and Central time zones (21% and 16% of classes) and rare in the Pacific zone (2%), where four-day timetables were more frequent (28%). Only 2 of all 1,998 detected classes met less than once a week, so the data say little about weekly or rarer classes.

Getting through the units

A class needed about 8 sessions for the lessons of unit 1 and 9 for unit 2, and 4–8 for later units, with large differences between classes. Few classes got far within the year: 717 finished unit 1's lessons, 297 unit 6 and 47 unit 11.

RQ2 · Meeting frequency

Per session, alternating-day and three-day classes covered the most, but they also had the longest sessions. Per class hour the timetables were close. Per school week, daily classes covered the most.

Holding session length, unit and time zone fixed, no timetable needed significantly more or fewer class hours per unit than daily classes. The weeks a unit took rose steeply as meetings became rarer.

Slightly more students kept up in classes that met more often: about 7 percentage points more per doubling of meetings per week between classes (p = .017). Session length made no difference.

RQ3 · Session length

Lessons per session doubled from 30–44-minute to 90-minute sessions, a session about 2.4 times as long. Lessons per class hour were highest for sessions under an hour.

Time zones

Pace was similar in the Eastern and Central zones; Pacific classes were somewhat slower, with overlapping intervals. The Mountain zone had only 12 classes with a measured pace.

Classes of the same teacher

Of 192 teachers with two or more measured classes, 17 taught classes on different timetables and 62 classes with different session lengths. Compared within the teacher, the weeks a unit took hardly changed with meetings per week or session length, so a teacher's classes with more meeting time spent more time per unit.

4 · Discussion

Total time matters; how it is split matters less

Frequency and spacing

Between two and five meetings a week, spreading the same class time over more or fewer meetings did not change how much class time a unit took, and daily classes were no less efficient per hour than three-day classes. Research on distributed practice concerns retention after a delay, which we did not measure; the keep-up result is the one sign in its direction.

Session length

Sessions longer than an hour covered more per session but used class time less efficiently. Fatigue fits this, but so do teachers using long blocks for practice or creative work that our lesson measure does not credit. For covering material, an hour in sessions under an hour was used at least as well as in long blocks.

Teachers pace by the calendar

A teacher's classes on different timetables moved through units in about the same number of weeks. Progress through the curriculum reflects the teacher's plan as much as students' learning, so measures of learning and comparisons within teachers are needed to isolate a timetable's effect.

For schools

How far students get depends on total weekly time more than on how it is divided. Shorter, more frequent sessions did not cost class time per unit, and more frequent meetings went with more students keeping up.

Story thread · The setting

This chapter studies US classes and has no self-efficacy measure, so it enters the story through the setting. Keeping up is the link: a student who has not finished a unit’s lessons when the class moves on starts the next unit without having finished the last. In the NTI cohorts, falling behind shows up as the slide of the least active students after unit 3 (Chapter 4) and as missed weeks that are hard to come back from (Chapter 3).

Limitations

  • Observational data: schools choose timetables and differ in other ways; only 17 teachers taught classes on different timetables.
  • Classes and sessions are inferred, and session length is platform time, not the school period; 56% of detected classes did not pass the quality rules.
  • Lesson progress and keeping up are not measures of learning or retention.
  • Weekly and rarer classes were too rare to study, and few classes reached units 9–12.
  • One course, one year, US schools; time zones assigned by state (12 states span two zones).

Teachers' and students' activity

1
HighThe classroom

How do teachers use the platform, and how does it relate to what their students do? Are more active teachers' students more active? Are there teachers with almost no activity? Most interesting: are teachers active at the same time as their students, and on the same exercises, as in live coding or a demonstration? While a teacher works through an exercise, do all students work on it too, or are some behind and watching while others are ahead on something else?

Questions, data, plan

Research questions

  • How much do teachers use the platform during class sessions, and how many teachers have minimal or no activity?
  • Is a teacher's activity associated with their students' activity, progress and keep-up?
  • When a teacher works on an exercise during class, what share of the students works on the same exercise at the same time, and how many are behind or ahead?
  • Do classes with more teacher–student synchrony have less spread between students, more students keeping up, or faster progress?

Data

The 2024–25 export has no teacher activity (see above), but the 2022–23 export (events_jul2022_aug2023_cs1-22.csv.gz, added 6 October) does: 9.8 million teacher events (user_role = t, 2,180 teachers, 886 of whom also teach in 2024–25), with teacher-only events such as share_to_classroom and set_last_task_id. What is still missing for 2022–23 is the student–teacher table that links students to their teachers. For 2024–25, a study needs a second export of teacher-account events with the same structure: page views, code runs, and if possible teacher-specific actions such as viewing the gradebook or a student's code, unlocking units, or presenting. Until then, a proxy study can use moments when many students open the same page within a minute or two, which suggests the teacher directed the class there.

Plan

  1. Request teacher events for the same teachers and year from CS Academy, and check whether the ethics approval covers teacher-level data.
  2. Link teacher events to classes by teacher ID and time: a teacher event inside a class session belongs to that session.
  3. Measures per session: teacher present (any event), teacher on the platform for what share of the session, teacher's page versus each student's page minute by minute.
  4. For teacher activity on an exercise, count students on the same exercise within a window of a few minutes, and students behind or ahead in course order (this reuses the positions from idea 2).
  5. Relate the measures to class progress, spread and keep-up, comparing classes of the same teacher where possible.
  6. Proxy study now: detect "class-wide openings" (for example at least half the present students opening the same notes page within two minutes) and study how often they happen and what follows.

Difficulty

High with teacher data (new export, approval, linking); about two months. The proxy study is Medium, about three weeks.

Who is ahead and who is behind during a class session

2
MediumThe classroom

During a session, students of a class are spread over the course: some on the page the class is working on, some still on earlier material, some ahead. How spread out are classes, does the spread grow over the year, and what happens to the students who fall behind?

Questions, data, plan

Research questions

  • How spread out are students' positions in the course during a session, and how does this change over the school year?
  • Are there students who are consistently ahead or behind their class?
  • Do classes that move in lockstep keep more students up than classes where students spread out?
  • How do students who fall behind catch up, if at all: during class, after school, or by skipping material?

Data

Available. The per-event context table gives each event's page; the course order of exercises and notes (ex_order, page_order) gives a position.

Plan

  1. For each student and 5-minute bin of a session, take their position as the furthest page in course order that they worked on in that bin (excluding pages they had already finished, to separate review from new work).
  2. Per bin: the class's median position and spread (interquartile range); per student: position relative to the class median.
  3. Per class: a lockstep index (mean spread over sessions) and its trend over the year; classify classes as teacher-paced or self-paced.
  4. Per student: how stable the relative position is across sessions (split-half reliability) and where catch-up work happens.
  5. Relate lockstep and relative position to keep-up, progress and errors.

Difficulty

Medium, about three to four weeks. The position table is large (hundreds of millions of events become tens of millions of student-bins), but per-state processing handles it.

First and last finishers in a class

3
Low to MediumThe classroom

In a class working on the same exercise, some students finish first, probably working it out on their own, and others finish last, perhaps after help from the teacher or classmates. Are these stable characteristics of students, and how do the processes of early and late finishers differ?

Questions, data, plan

Research questions

  • Are some students consistently among the first, or the last, in their class to finish exercises?
  • How do early and late finishers differ in their process: errors, runs, pauses, time on task?
  • Do late finishes show signs of help, such as a long pause followed by a quick correct submission?
  • Is finishing order associated with later progress, keep-up and persistence?

Data

Available: first completions with timestamps, class membership and sessions.

Plan

  1. For each class and exercise with at least five classmates finishing it in the same session, compute each student's percentile rank of finishing time, measured from when the student started the exercise and from the session start.
  2. Per student: mean percentile and its reliability across exercises.
  3. Process features per attempt from the context table: errors and runs before success, longest pause, time from the last error to success.
  4. Models with class fixed effects relating finishing order to process features and later outcomes.

Difficulty

Low to Medium, about two weeks.

Error patterns across units

4
MediumLearning, errors and time

Which errors do students make, how does the mix change as the course introduces new concepts, and how long does it take to get past each kind of error? Python's built-in errors and the platform's own messages (for example about invalid shape properties) can be compared.

Questions, data, plan

Research questions

  • Which compile and runtime errors are most common in each unit, and how does the mix change across the course?
  • How long do students take to resolve each kind of error, and which messages are followed by quick fixes versus long struggles?
  • Are students' error profiles in the first units associated with their later progress?
  • Which exercises produce more errors than their difficulty would suggest?

Data

Available. Full messages are in the raw events (info); the per-state extracts truncate them to 80 characters, so the error events need a fresh extract (about 21 million rows).

Plan

  1. Extract all compile_error and runtime_error events with full message, student, time and exercise context.
  2. Normalise messages: strip line numbers, names and values; group into types (exception class plus a pattern, for example "expected an indented block", "not callable", platform property checks).
  3. Per unit and exercise: errors per run and the distribution of types.
  4. Resolution time: time from an error until the next successful run or autograde pass on the same exercise.
  5. Student error profiles in units 1–3 and their relation to later progress, with class fixed effects.

Difficulty

Medium, about three to four weeks, most of it in normalising messages.

Choosing easy or hard exercises

5
MediumMotivation and engagement

CS Academy exercises are worth different numbers of points, roughly reflecting difficulty. Do some students go for hard (3-point) exercises from the start, while others stick to easy (1-point) ones? Are there students who start with easy exercises and later move on to hard ones, and what does that say about their confidence and progress?

Questions, data, plan

Research questions

  • When a section offers exercises of different difficulty, do students follow the listed order or choose by difficulty?
  • Which trajectories exist: easy throughout, hard throughout, easy first then hard, hard first then easy?
  • Are the trajectories associated with persistence, progress and keep-up?
  • How much of the choice is the student's and how much the teacher's (assigned subsets)?

Data

Point values are not in the data and need to be added: ask CS Academy for an exercise list with points, or compile it from the course pages. Empirical difficulty is available already: median active minutes to solve (the effort weights), share solved on the first try, and share of attempters who finish.

Plan

  1. Obtain point values and compare them with empirical difficulty.
  2. Identify choice situations: collections where a student had several unsolved exercises available and attempted them out of order, and classes where students worked on different subsets.
  3. Per student and unit: share of attempts and completions on hard exercises.
  4. Cluster students' sequences across units into trajectories (for example with sequence clustering or a simple rule-based typology).
  5. Relate trajectories to persistence (idea 8) and to progress, comparing students within the same class.

Difficulty

Medium, about three to four weeks; Low if a list of point values is easy to get.

Window focus and blur as signals of attention

6
MediumMotivation and engagement

Window focus and blur events record when the CS Academy window gains and loses focus. Patterns of leaving and returning may indicate attention, distraction or looking things up elsewhere. Can they predict how far students get, beyond time on task?

Questions, data, plan

Research questions

  • How often, and for how long, do students leave the CS Academy window during class sessions?
  • Does leaving become more frequent late in long sessions, after long breaks, or late in the school year?
  • Do attention features predict progress (for example total points, points from 3-point exercises, or completions, per unit or for units 1–6 and 7–12) beyond time on task?
  • Are there distinct attention profiles among students?

Data

Available. Points are not (see idea 5); until they are, completions or effort-weighted credit can stand in. First check what triggers a blur: a blur followed by code runs without a focus event, for example, would suggest that clicking into the running program's canvas also counts as a blur.

Plan

  1. Validate the meaning of blur and focus with event sequences and, if possible, with CS Academy.
  2. Per student and session: blurs per active hour, time away (blur to next focus), share of the session away, and when in the session it happens.
  3. Aggregate per student and unit range.
  4. Predict outcomes with cross-validation that holds out whole teachers (so that the model does not learn teacher-specific patterns), comparing models with and without attention features.
  5. Look at how attention changes within long sessions, which connects to the session-length results of the paper.

Difficulty

Medium, about three weeks.

Creative tasks, motivation and work outside class

7
MediumMotivation and engagement

Each unit ends with open-ended creative and collaborative tasks. Some classes do them in every unit, others rarely. Does time spent on creative tasks early in the course (say units 1–3) go with more time on later creative tasks, as if students who get into programming become motivated to do more? Or does engagement fade? And do engaged students also work outside class time, in the evening and at weekends?

Questions, data, plan

Research questions

  • How do classes differ in their use of creative tasks: every unit, some units, none?
  • Does a student's time on early creative tasks predict their time on later ones, compared with classmates?
  • Do students who invest in early creative tasks do more work outside school hours (after 5 pm, at weekends) later in the course?
  • Is creative engagement associated with persistence and progress on the core exercises?

Data

Available. The pipeline already measures active minutes, runs and edits per student and task, and "real work" on a task (at least 5 active minutes, 3 runs and 10 edits). Local timestamps give school-time versus outside-time work. In the California pilot, about 15% of completions happened outside 07:00–16:00 on weekdays.

Plan

  1. Per class: share of units in which most students worked on creative tasks; classify classes.
  2. Per student and unit: creative minutes, inside and outside class sessions and school hours.
  3. Within classes that assign creative tasks throughout, model later creative engagement on early engagement with class fixed effects, and look for typical trajectories (growing, steady, fading).
  4. Relate early creative engagement to later out-of-class work and to sandbox programming (idea 13).

Difficulty

Medium, about three to four weeks.

Persistence: finishing exercises that take longer than usual

8
MediumMotivation and engagement

Some students keep going when an exercise takes much longer than it usually does, and finish it; others give up. With a typical solving time for every exercise, each attempt can be placed relative to it: did the student finish although they were well past the typical time, or did they stop?

Questions, data, plan

Research questions

  • How often do students abandon exercises they have started, and after how much time relative to the typical solving time?
  • Are some students consistently persistent, finishing even when they need much longer than usual?
  • Is persistence associated with later progress, keep-up and creative engagement?
  • Do the class context and timetable matter: do students give up more often when a session ends during an attempt, or in classes with short sessions?

Data

Available. The pipeline computes, for each exercise, the typical active time to the first correct solution (the effort weights, from a model that adjusts for which students attempt the exercise), and for each student and exercise the active time spent, the runs and edits, and whether and when it was completed. In the California pilot (before the active-time fix of 5 October), students worked on 178,704 exercises with at least one run or edit and never completed 30,586 of them (17%); a median of 6.2 active minutes went into each of those, and they took 17% of all time on autograded exercises. The completion rate of attempted exercises fell from 93% in unit 1 to 66–71% in units 5–6.

Plan

  1. Fit the typical solving time per exercise once on all states, so it is on the same scale everywhere.
  2. Define an attempt (at least one run or edit) and its outcome: completed, or abandoned (no completion by the end of the data). Treat an exercise as assigned when most classmates completed it, to separate giving up from "the teacher never assigned it".
  3. Relative time: time spent divided by the typical time; mark attempts that went beyond, for example, the 75th percentile of solving times.
  4. Persistence of a student: the share of long attempts that ended in completion, or better a student effect in a model of completion given time spent, with exercise and class effects.
  5. Survival view: the chance of finishing versus stopping as time on an attempt accumulates.
  6. Relate persistence to later outcomes and to ideas 5 and 7.

Difficulty

Medium, about three weeks.

Forgetting over breaks, measured properly

10
Medium to HighLearning, errors and time

The first California analysis of gaps between sessions was not convincing: the gap measured days since the class last used the platform together, irregular pauses dominated, and progress mixed several activities. A cleaner study would separate timetable gaps (weekends, alternating days) from irregular pauses and use outcomes closer to forgetting.

Questions, data, plan

Research questions

  • After longer timetable gaps and school breaks, are students' first minutes back slower or more error-prone?
  • Do students answer checkpoint questions or redo earlier material less well after a break?
  • How long does it take for a class to get back to its usual pace after winter or spring break?

Data

Available: sessions with low-use days, the reviewers' labels for timetable versus irregular gaps (in notes/review_2026-10-02/measurement-validity), error and run events, notes checkpoint answers with first-try correctness.

Plan

  1. Label each gap as timetable (weekend, alternating day), holiday or break (confirmed by all of the teacher's classes being silent), or irregular pause.
  2. Outcomes for the first session after a gap: errors per run in the first ten minutes, time to the first completion, first-try correctness on checkpoints, time spent on earlier pages.
  3. Within-class and within-student models, with the session before the gap as a placebo comparison.
  4. Event studies around Thanksgiving, winter break and spring break.

Difficulty

Medium to High, about four to six weeks.

Teachers' choices of what to teach

Low to MediumThe classroom

Students complete only part of each unit's exercises: in the California pilot, the median student completed 57% of the exercises below their furthest point in the course, which suggests that teachers assign subsets. Which exercises and tasks do teachers select or skip, and how do these choices relate to pace and keep-up?

Questions, data, plan

Research questions

  • Which exercises, creative tasks and assessments do classes skip, and are there typical coverage patterns?
  • Do teachers who cover more of each unit move more slowly, and do their students keep up better?

Data

Available: which exercises most of a class completed, per class and unit.

Plan

Class-by-exercise coverage matrix; cluster classes by coverage; relate coverage patterns to pace, keep-up and assessment results; feed findings back to curriculum designers.

Difficulty

Low to Medium, about two weeks.
