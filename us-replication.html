The cohort findings at US scale

The findings of the earlier chapters come from about 150 students in the five TE22 and TE23 classes in Sweden, and in places from a sixth, te24c. Here each one is tested again on 16,909 US students who took the same CS1 course on the same platform, with the rules for every test fixed before it ran. How students program, fix errors and keep pace holds at scale. The student profiles turn out to be regions of one continuous population, and a few classroom findings stay local to the Swedish classes.

The programming process is the same

The 64 transitions between eight programming actions have almost the same probabilities in the US as in the Swedish classes (r = .98): an edit is followed by a run without an error 68% of the time in both. The loop and list units change the process the same way.

Errors and effort hold

One attempt in five ends in an error (19.6%), most are fixed within 14.5 seconds, and the specific syntax messages get faster to fix with practice while "invalid syntax" does not. 83% of struggled attempts are still solved.

Falling behind, and the teacher

US students who are usually behind fall further behind (74% against 15% of the rest). Pace spread belongs to the teacher’s classes, but at this scale so do errors and lesson accuracy: the "teacher effect" is teacher plus school.

Profiles are regions, not types

The cohorts’ student profiles mostly reappear among the US students, but none survives resampling the classes, and the data hold no more structure than one population. The exception is whether a student works outside lessons at all.

What stays local

Leaving the platform together during lessons is not general (3% of classes, below chance), and the two grade links seen only in TE23 have no US counterpart. Grades, surveys and code are not in the US data, so those chapters stay cohort-only.

What was tested, and how

The US data are one school year (July 2024 to June 2025) of CMU CS Academy’s event log for the same CS1 course the Swedish classes used. They hold no grades, surveys, code or prior-experience answers, and classes and lessons are not recorded: they are inferred from which students work at the same times. The sample is the 16,909 students placed in one of 871 clean inferred classes, taught by 498 teachers in 49 states.

Each finding was tested in one of eight batches. Before each batch ran, a dated note fixed its definitions (carried over from the cohort code) and the rule for its verdict. An effect is supported when it goes the cohorts’ way, its 95% interval from resampling whole classes excludes zero, it is at least half the cohorts’ size, and it holds at that size in at least two thirds of the 11 states with 20 or more classes. Descriptive values must fall in a band around the cohort value. A finding is not supported when the US interval lies on the other side; anything in between is inconclusive. Grade links are tested against course progress as a stand-in for the grade and are marked as proxies.

Verdicts, finding by finding

27 of the 42 findings below are supported, 3 partly, 7 inconclusive, 4 not supported and 1 cannot be tested. For the profile analyses, "partly" means the profiles mostly reappear but not as stable groups, which is what the cohort analyses claimed; "not supported" means a claim about the structure fails (the pathways claimed distinct clusters, the activity clusters a continuum).

The programming process is the same

An earlier Markov model of the programming process describes each student’s work as a chain of eight actions: navigating, editing, running without an error, a compile or runtime error, a correct or incorrect check, and a reset. Built the same way on 78.7 million US transitions, its 64 probabilities lie close to the cohort values (r = .98). The largest gap: after an incorrect check, US students more often simply run the code again without an error.

In the loop and list units (7–10 and the final project) the process changes in the same way in both data sets, within the same 4,071 US students: more repeated failed checks, more runs straight after a clean run, and more runtime errors after an edit (+.05, cohorts +.05). The eight largest changes have the same sign in both.

Activity over the course: the same three shapes

Chapter 4 grouped students by how active they were in each unit. The same method on the 1,206 US students in the 62 classes that finish the course finds the same three trajectories: a large group active throughout, a middle group, and a low group that falls off early in the course and comes back for the final project (mean state 1.10 in units 8–10, 2.11 in unit 12). The US middle group has a clearer shape than the cohorts’: active until unit 6, then a drop at the for-loops in unit 7.

As in the cohorts, activity is fairly stable from unit to unit (ICC .63), and who lands in which trajectory depends on how the activity states are defined (agreement ARI .38 between definitions). The shapes are a description of where activity falls off, not fixed types of student.

Student profiles are regions of one population

Four cohort analyses sorted students into profiles: error profiles (Chapter 6), activity clusters, pace and workload profiles, and the error-quotient × time-on-task pathways of a paper on two TE22 classes (Snider, Bosk & Bälter, FIE 2026). Fitted to the US students with the same features and the same number of groups, most cohort profiles have a US counterpart with the same defining features, sometimes almost exactly. But no set of profiles comes back reliably when the classes are resampled or the fit is repeated within single states, and the groups separate no better than groups cut from a single population with the same distributions. This extends Chapter 5’s one-population result to every clustering in the project.

The exception is the activity clusters. US students split mainly into those who work outside lessons and those who barely do, partly because whole classes differ in how much work goes home. For the FIE pathways, the paper’s summed unit EQ turns out to track how many exercises a student ran (ρ .60–.88), so its EQ and time features largely measure the same volume.

What stays local to the Swedish classes

Leaving the platform together. In TE23 and te24c the students most often away from the platform during lessons tended to leave at the same moments. Across 710 US classes this happens in 3.2%, below the 5% expected by chance: it is a pattern of those classes, not of students who are often away.

The TE23-only grade links. Working outside lessons and time away from the platform went with the grade only in TE23. Against course progress in the US, both are near zero (.08 and −.07), like TE22’s values. The three grade links that held in every cohort (minutes per point, harder exercises, working ahead) have US counterparts with the same sign, but most of each runs through how far a student had already got.

Shared sticking points. In TE23, 88% of students who gave up on an exercise stopped on a test that a classmate had also failed and then passed. Within US classes it is 74%, because US classes are smaller: pooled over a teacher’s classes, the share rises with the pool and reaches the cohort value at the cohort’s size.

Method

Data
CMU CS Academy’s US event log for 2024/25 (345 million events), used under the platform’s research agreement; only aggregates are shown. Classes, lessons and terms are inferred from co-activity by the timetable pipeline behind Chapter 8.
Sample
Students placed in one of the 871 clean classes (16,909 students, one class each). Some tests use subsets: the 62 classes that finish the course (trajectories), students with enough data in each unit or stage, and for prior experience the classes that hold students from 2022/23.
Definitions
Carried over from the cohort code and listed, with every change, in the batch notes in the project's analysis repository (private; available on request).
Verdicts
Fixed before each run. Effects: the cohorts’ direction, a 95% class-bootstrap interval excluding zero, at least half the cohorts’ size, and the same in at least two thirds of the 11 states with 20 or more classes. Descriptive values: a band around the cohort value. Profiles: recurrence of the cohort profiles’ defining features and comparison with a one-population null.
Proxies
Grade links use end-of-term progress through the class’s lesson items, ranked within class, with the predictor taken from the first half of the term.
Post-hoc checks
Run after the fixed analyses and marked as such (the pool-size table, the EQ volume check); they never change a verdict.
