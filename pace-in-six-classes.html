Keeping up with the class, in all six classes

On each class day, how far is a student ahead of or behind the class median in lesson pages and checkpoints? Measured the same way in the five TE22 and TE23 classes and in te24c, this lead goes with the final grade in every class. How far the students spread out depends on the class: in one teacher's two classes most students moved with the median, in the other four fewer than half did.

The spread follows the class

In teacher 1's two classes 75% of students stayed around the class median; in teacher 2's three classes 33% and in te24c 28%. The middle half of a class spans about 2.1 lesson items in teacher 1's classes, 4.5 in teacher 2's and 7.1 in te24c.

Ahead goes with the grade

In every class, students further ahead of the median got higher final grades (ρ .39 to .77; all six pooled .60 [.49, .69]). Students usually ahead average 4.1, those around the median 2.9 and those usually behind 1.9 (A = 5, F = 0).

Self-efficacy, less clearly

Pooled, the lead goes with self-efficacy after the course too (ρ .43 [.27, .57]), but within a single class the interval excludes zero only in te22c and te23c.

Few catch up

28 students fell further behind in the second half of the course, 21 of them already usually behind. Only 5 caught up or pulled ahead, all in te23b and te24c.

Read with care

Six classes cannot separate teacher, school year and class. The links are associations: being ahead may reflect prior experience as much as work.

How far apart the classes spread

On each class day, every student's count of completed lesson pages and checkpoints is compared with the class median that day. A student above the median on at least two thirds of the days is usually ahead, one below it on at least two thirds is usually behind, and everyone else is around the median.

Teacher 1's two classes moved in step: 78% and 72% of the students stayed around the median, and the middle half of each class was spread over about two items (interquartile range 2.2 and 1.9). In teacher 2's classes fewer than half stayed around the median in both years, fewest in te23b, where only 19% stayed around the median and the rest split almost evenly into 14 ahead and 15 behind. te24c spread the most (interquartile range 7.1 items) over 19 class days, against 43–50 in the other classes.

This repeats the teacher pattern of Chapter 10 and adds te24c. Whether it is the teacher's way of running the lessons, the year or the students cannot be told apart with six classes.

Being ahead goes with the grade, in every class

In all six classes, students further ahead of the median got higher final grades. The correlation ranges from .39 in te23c, whose final grade is the final-project grade, to .77 in te22c. Pooled over the six classes it is .60 [.49, .69], and every interval is above zero.

The pace groups show the same ladder: students usually ahead average a grade of 4.1, those around the median 2.9 and those usually behind 1.9. The groups differ in every class (Kruskal–Wallis p < .05), and the usually-behind group has the lowest mean grade everywhere. The one break in the order is te23b, where the 5 students around the median average 4.2 against 4.0 for those ahead.

Self-efficacy follows less clearly

Students further ahead also reported higher self-efficacy after the course (pooled ρ .43 [.27, .57]), but less clearly than the grade. Within a single class the interval excludes zero only in te22c (.71) and te23c (.59); with 15–24 survey answers per class the other intervals are wide. In te24c, surveyed in August 2026, it is .35 [−.09, .72]. The te24c chapter adds that students usually ahead gained the most self-efficacy over the course (Chapter 9).

As with errors (Chapter 6) and unit trajectories (Chapter 4), the log goes with the grade more than with how capable students feel. TE22 answered the survey a year after its course, and all of these are associations, not effects.

Falling behind, and the few who catch up

Comparing each student's mean lead in the second half of the course with the first, 28 students fell behind by more than 4.7 items, and 21 of them were already usually behind. Only 5 caught up or pulled ahead, 3 in te23b and 2 in te24c. Falling behind was most common in te23b (9), te24c (8) and te22a (5), the three classes with the fewest students around the median.

Students who fell behind ended lower in their class's grade order than those who held their position (mean grade percentile .41 against .53; .76 for the 5 who caught up). te24c is the exception: its 8 students who fell behind averaged a grade of 3.7, against 3.5 for those who held their position.

Against the earlier chapters

Chapter 9 and Chapter 10 measured pace with the te24c analysis scripts, one class at a time. This chapter applies the same rules to all six classes from the current exports. The results agree with the saved per-student tables behind those chapters: every student's lead is within 1.1 items of the saved value, 193 of 194 students keep their pace group and 186 their change between the halves. The lesson items differ: 61 in every class here, 39 (units 2–7) in the te24c chapter, which is why te24c's counts of students falling behind differ slightly.

Chapter 10 reports ρ .65 for TE22 and .54 for TE23 between the lead and the final grade. Those values come from the per-class grade tables of the TE22/TE23 analysis, with 91 and 67 students. After the consent rules used here, 79 and 63 remain, and the values are .70 and .57.

Method

Lesson items
Lesson pages and checkpoints (notes exercises) from unit 2 on that at least 5 students in the class completed: 61 items in every class. Completion times come from each class's platform export; TE23's are shifted back one hour to Swedish time.
Class day
A day on which at least 10 students of the class completed something, counted from the start of the course: 20 August 2023 (TE22), 19 August 2024 (TE23) and 13 January 2026 (te24c, after two introductory lessons in November). 43–50 days in TE22 and TE23, 19 in te24c.
Lead over the class median
On each class day, the number of lesson items the student had completed minus the class median that day, averaged over the class days.
Pace group
Usually ahead: above the median on at least two thirds of the class days. Usually behind: below it on at least two thirds. Around the median: everyone else.
Spread
Interquartile range of the lead within the class: how many items separate the student a quarter of the way from the top and the one a quarter of the way from the bottom.
Fell behind, caught up
Mean lead in the second half of the course minus the first, beyond ±4.7 items (the te24c rule of 3 items out of 39, scaled to 61).
Grades
StudyBee final grade, A = 5 … F = 0. te23c's final grade is its final-project grade, as in the other chapters.
Self-efficacy
Mean of the 15 core items (1–7). TE22 and TE23 answered in May–June 2025, a year after TE22's course and at the end of TE23's; te24c answered in August 2026.
Consent
Grades are left out for students who declined StudyBee data, and pace for the 6 who declined CS Academy data. te24c's survey did not ask either question; its data are used as in Chapter 9.
Statistics
Spearman ρ with 2000-resample bootstrap 95% intervals; pooled values use percentile ranks within each class. Pace groups are compared with Kruskal–Wallis tests on grade ranks within the class.
Built from the platform exports with analyses/pace/pace_all.py, pace_grades.py and pace_page.py (cs-academy-analysis), using the grades and survey table of the SCT replication (Chapter 12).
