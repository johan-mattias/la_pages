Exploratory checks and next steps

Four questions from reading the story closely, checked in the logs of the same five classes, and the work they point to. The checks were not planned before the data were seen, they run many tests, and self-efficacy was measured once, after the course. Read them as leads for the next study, not as findings of this one. Two of them already shape other chapters: their measures are features of Chapter 2, and a third corrected how Chapter 3 reads reruns after errors.

Choosing harder exercises

In social cognitive theory, self-efficacy shapes which tasks people take on. Exercises are worth 1, 2 or 3 points, and most exercises beyond the checkpoints are optional, so the share of a student’s attempted exercises worth 2 points or more shows what they chose. Students solve almost everything they attempt, so the share is about choice rather than success.

The share goes with self-efficacy and with the final grade. It also goes with doing more exercises, but the link stays when the number attempted is held constant, and again when the Year 1 block/text test is held constant as well. It does not go with self-efficacy for the advanced Structures items (.08) or with programming outside school before the course (.14). The level chosen in units 1–3 points the same way, but with volume and the test score held constant it is no longer clear. Whether choosing hard tasks is a sign of self-efficacy or a source of mastery experience cannot be told apart here; the theory expects both.

Effort against results

Some students seem to do well with little activity and others poorly with a lot. In the logs that is rare. Active minutes and the final grade go together (ρ .54 [.41, .66], n = 140). Counting each class in thirds, 14 students sit in the two mismatch corners (top third for effort and bottom third for the grade, or the reverse), against about 31 if effort and the grade were unrelated.

Beyond effort, how students work still matters. With active minutes held constant, working ahead of the class median, fixing errors at the first retry and choosing harder exercises go with a higher grade; leaving exercises unsolved and giving up on errors with a lower one. Self-efficacy adds to effort too. Attending lessons and working outside them do not, once total effort is counted: how much and how, not where.

Persistence at the wall

Units 8–10 are where the course gets hard: the exercises with the lowest pass rates and the lowest self-ratings point to the same wall (Chapter 3). The wall is real but not total: 144 of 153 students attempted something there, and the median student solved 91% of the exercises they attempted, against 97% in units 1–7. Of the exercises they struggled with (at least one incorrect check), they solved 80%, against 92%.

Students with higher self-efficacy attempt more of units 8–10, also with their attempts in units 1–7 held constant, and they try longer before leaving an exercise unsolved. That is persistence as the theory describes it. But they do not clearly solve more of the exercises they struggle with (.16 [−.07, .39]); the grade does (.33). Persistence alone does not get them over the wall.

Concept checkpoints as markers

Which single exercises go with self-efficacy and the grade? The checkpoints are the fairest place to look, because nearly every student does them: 24 checkpoints have at least 60 students, against few of the optional exercises. Per student and checkpoint: minutes, runs, errors, incorrect checks and a correct first check. 26 of 266 tests pass the false-discovery correction.

Time on a checkpoint goes with a lower grade and lower self-efficacy (median ρ −.21 and −.11), and the strongest links sit on the checkpoints that introduce a new concept: events, conditionals, key presses and step events, in units 2–6. The unit 1 drawing checkpoints say little about self-efficacy (median ρ −.07; none passes the correction). Error measures are weaker than time and runs. A student who needs long on the checkpoint that introduces events is more likely to end with a lower grade and lower self-efficacy, which makes these short checkpoints useful as early markers of where a new concept is hard. Chapter 2 uses the time on them as a feature.

Already in the other chapters

Reruns after errors. Edits are logged coarsely, so “ran again without editing” cannot be read from the logged edits. Defined by timing instead, a quick rerun within 3 seconds of an error is a habit of the student, mostly re-pressing run after a syntax error, and it goes with neither the grade nor self-efficacy. What goes with a lower grade is the next run showing the same message, at any delay. Chapter 3 reads its transitions this way, and Chapter 2 uses the same-message share as a feature.

Lessons in all five classes. Chapter 7 follows te22a’s lessons; the same pipeline now runs for every class. Fewer students are fully active per lesson in spring than in autumn in all five, while attendance hardly moves. In the TE22 classes most of the work outside lessons goes to the creative tasks and the final project (61%–83%), in the TE23 classes less than half (42%–47%).

Next steps

  1. Measure the change. Self-efficacy was asked once. A second survey of the same students would turn the story’s inferences about improvement into measured change; te24c, surveyed before and after, is the first small test (Chapter 2).
  2. Mastery experience as a process. Abstract each exercise and lesson page into states (selecting it, programming with or without a known error, a correct or incorrect check, a break, the documentation, finishing or leaving; reading, trying code and answering questions on lesson pages) and compare the sequences of students whose practice goes with self-efficacy with those whose practice does not. It would show what mastery experience is in the logs, beyond the edit–run–error transitions of Chapter 3.
  3. The checks above on te24c, the class surveyed before and after: choosing harder exercises and persistence at the wall, with change as the outcome.
  4. One model for the grade: effort plus how students work (working ahead, fixing at the first retry, harder exercises, unsolved exercises), with the classes as a factor.
  5. Prior experience: how the Year 1 block/text test and programming before the course relate to self-efficacy and the grade, and whether students who arrive behind catch up (Chapter 1, Chapter 12).
  6. Unit trajectories against the survey: whether the activity trajectories of Chapter 4 differ in goals, help seeking and habits.
  7. The rest of te24c’s year: its log ends in unit 4; a later export would cover the whole course.
  8. At US scale: the checks that need no survey (harder exercises against the grade, the wall, the concept checkpoints) on the 16,909 US students of Chapter 14.

Method

Students
The five TE22 and TE23 classes, each cohort’s own course year. Students who declined the use of their CS Academy data are left out, grades of those who declined StudyBee data, and survey answers are used only with consent.
Self-efficacy
Mean of the 15 core items (1–7), answered in May–June 2025: at the end of TE23’s course and about eleven months after TE22’s. Every link with it is an association with a later self-report.
Correlations
Spearman ρ on percentile ranks within each class, pooled over the classes, with 2000-resample bootstrap 95% intervals. “Held constant”: partial rank correlations. Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery correction within each question.
Harder exercises
Exercises with any check result, worth 2 or more points, as a share of all attempted exercises (class medians 27–38%).
Struggled, tries before quitting
Struggled: at least one incorrect check on the exercise. Tries before quitting: incorrect checks on exercises never solved, median per student.
Checkpoint measures
Minutes from the platform export; runs, the share of runs ending in an error and incorrect checks from the log.
Exploratory analyses of the proofreading questions, run on the consented data. Code and notes are in the project’s analysis repository (private; available on request).
