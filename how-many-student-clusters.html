One population, not groups

One. In both windows, the full course and units 3–11, the methods of chapters 8 and 9 of Learning Analytics Methods and Tutorials (Saqr & López-Pernas 2024) agree that the students form a single continuous cloud. Model-based clustering still splits it, but along continuous dimensions rather than into kinds of student: over the full course by where students do their work, in units 3–11 by how far ahead of the class they are, plus a handful who barely worked there. The factors are those of Chapter 2’s revised analysis; the at-risk tail found with its first factors does not come back.

Book ch. 8 · k-means, PAM and hierarchical clustering

No value of k separates the students

The silhouette width measures how much closer each student is to their own cluster than to the nearest other one. Across k = 2 to 8 and all three algorithms it never gets past . For k = 2 to 4 it sits inside the range the same data gives when it is simulated as one population. The gap statistic picks K = 1, and the elbow curve bends smoothly with no kink.

mclust prefers two components

Extra checks

Why two components are not two groups

A mixture of two normal curves can describe one skewed, heavy-tailed cloud just as well as two separate groups. These checks tell the two apart.

    Solution

    What this means

    Read the factors as dimensions, not types

    • No distinct subgroups. In both windows silhouette widths are low at every k and no better than a one-population simulation, the gap statistic picks one cluster, and the two-component mixture has one peak.
    • The splits follow dimensions, not types. Over the full course the two mclust components differ most in where students do their work: coming back outside lessons, against presence in them. In units 3–11 the best mixture separates a handful of students who barely worked there, and splits the rest along how far ahead of the class they are; without that factor one component wins. A person-centred summary should name the dimension, not the group.
    • The first version’s at-risk tail does not come back. With chapter 2’s first factors the smaller component was a spread-out tail of sparse, error-prone students, resting largely on one sessions factor. On the revised factors no component is set apart by errors, and the two windows’ components are different students.
    • The factors work best as continuous dimensions, as in the self-efficacy analyses of chapter 2. Comparing clusters on self-efficacy would need a theoretical reason, since the factors were chosen partly because they predict it.
    • Still open: heavy-tailed or skewed mixtures (multivariate t, skew-normal) would test the tail reading directly.
    Story thread · Stalls and drops

    Other chapters group students too. The four working styles in Chapter 3 separate on outcomes but are not discrete types, the start-and-finish profiles in Chapter 1 are descriptive, and the activity trajectories in Chapter 4 recur in shape but not in membership. This chapter asks the question directly and finds one continuous population, so read every profile on the site as a region of that continuum. The story’s drops are not a separate group either: on the revised factors no component is set apart by errors or sparse activity, and the splits follow where students work and how far ahead of the class they are.

    Data. students in two cohorts, without the students who declined the use of their CS Academy data. The factor scores are the family factors of Chapter 2’s revised analysis, for the full course and for units 3–11 (Chapter 2’s best window from the end). Clustering uses mastery experience, self-regulation without its weak “away and switching” factor, persistence and debugging without its volume factor (“errors met”), and social comparison (“ahead of the class”): factors for the full course and for units 3–11. Effort, work strategy, the trajectory, the lesson questions and creative work are left out, as volume, weak or full-course only. All factors were z-scored, and students inactive in the later window were dropped (). No self-efficacy comparisons are shown here. A leave-one-factor-out check refits each window without each factor in turn.

    Methods. R 4.5.2 with mclust 6.1.2, cluster 2.1.8.2 and diptest 0.77.2, following book ch. 8 and ch. 9. The one-population simulations use a Gaussian copula with each factor's exact empirical distribution; the three-peak check also draws from the fitted two-component VEE model and refits the real data without six random students. Peaks are counted with modal EM (Li, Ray & Lindsay 2007).
