One population, not groups

One. In both time windows, the methods of chapters 8 and 9 of Learning Analytics Methods and Tutorials (Saqr & López-Pernas 2024) agree that the students form a single continuous cloud. Model-based clustering adds a second, smaller profile, but it is a spread-out tail of less regular, more error-prone students around the same centre, not a separate group. From week 8/9 the best mixture has three peaks, but they rarely survive leaving out a few students.

Verdict

Book ch. 8 · k-means, PAM and hierarchical clustering

No value of k separates the students

The silhouette width measures how much closer each student is to their own cluster than to the nearest other one. Across k = 2 to 8 and all three algorithms it never gets past . For k = 2 to 4 it sits inside the range the same data gives when it is simulated as one population. The gap statistic picks K = 1, and the elbow curve bends smoothly with no kink.

Book ch. 9 · Gaussian mixture models with mclust

mclust prefers two components

Book ch. 9 fits Gaussian mixtures with 1 to 9 components under 14 covariance structures and compares them by BIC and ICL, where higher is better. For VEE with two components, entropy () and the average posterior probabilities (at least ) the book chapter's thresholds of 0.6 and 0.8.

Extra checks

Why two components are not two groups

A mixture of two normal curves can describe one skewed, heavy-tailed cloud just as well as two separate groups. These checks tell the two apart.

    Solution

    What this means

    Read the factors as dimensions, not types

    • No distinct subgroups. Silhouette widths are low at every k and no better than a one-population simulation, the gap statistic picks one cluster, and the core-and-tail mixture has one peak. The three peaks of the best mixture from week 8/9 do not hold up when a few students are left out.
    • A person-centred summary is a majority plus a tail. The two-profile mclust solution describes a typical majority and a smaller at-risk tail with sparse, error-prone activity, not two kinds of student.
    • The factors work best as continuous dimensions, as in the self-efficacy analyses of chapter 2. Comparing clusters on self-efficacy would need a theoretical reason, since the factors were chosen partly because they predict it.
    • Still open: heavy-tailed or skewed mixtures (multivariate t, skew-normal) would test the tail reading directly.
    Story thread · Stalls and drops

    Other chapters group students too. The four working styles in Chapter 3 separate on outcomes but are not discrete types, the start-and-finish profiles in Chapter 1 are descriptive, and the activity trajectories in Chapter 4 recur in shape but not in membership. This chapter asks the question directly and finds one continuous population, so read every profile on the site as a region of that continuum. Its tail, sparse activity with error-ended sessions and high error proneness, is where the story's drops sit.

    Data. students in two cohorts, without the students who declined the use of their CS Academy data. Clustering uses the factors marked for clustering in the factor key: sessions, progress and mastery, active-day regularity, and debugging error proneness. That is 10 factors for the full course and 11 for weeks 8/9 to end. All factors were z-scored, and the two students inactive in the later window were dropped. No self-efficacy comparisons are shown here.

    Methods. R 4.3.3 with mclust 6.1.2 and cluster 2.1.6, following book ch. 8 and ch. 9. The one-population simulations use a Gaussian copula with each factor's exact empirical distribution; the three-peak check also draws from the fitted core-and-tail model and refits the real data without six random students. Peaks are counted with modal EM (Li, Ray & Lindsay 2007).
