Source material

Six files behind the page

Two event logs, an account map that links each platform account to its survey answers and class, the exercise catalogue, and the two survey exports. Students who declined the use of their CS Academy data are left out of the logs and the map, and survey answers are used only with the student’s consent. All 96 survey answers used link to an account with log data.

The two logs do not share events. 38 students appear in both because they continued across course instances, but their events are distinct.

57 of the 153 logged students have no survey answer that may be used. Their classes are known from the platform exports, and their median activity (13,500 events, against 15,500 for the respondents) is similar. Every behaviour-to-self-efficacy result on this page therefore rests on the 96 students with both, about two thirds of the logged population.

Book ch. 7 · Predictive modelling

How early can you see it coming?

The question an early-warning system has to answer: using only what a student did in their first n days, can you tell who ends the year in the upper half for exercises passed? Features are computed inside the window only; the outcome is the full-year count. Five-fold stratified cross-validation, 153 students, base rate 49.0%.

Two weeks of data already reach AUC 0.82 with a linear model. The curve then climbs slowly — the extra ten weeks between day 14 and day 84 buy about 0.1 AUC. For an intervention that has to happen while there is still time to act, the information is almost all present by the end of the first month.

Watch the target definition. The same model with whole-course features predicting the whole-course outcome scores AUC 0.92, almost entirely because the number of submissions and the number of exercises passed are the same construct measured twice. Restricting features to an early window is what makes the number mean something. Leakage in log data usually looks like a feature that is a component of the outcome rather than a cause of it.

Two process features survive alongside the volume measures. Errors per run correlates −0.35 with final exercises passed: students who trigger a compile or runtime error on a larger share of their executions finish behind. Runs per edit is similar at −0.29. Edits are logged coarsely (see the transitions below), so read it as how often students re-run between logged edits rather than as unchanged code. Neither is a volume measure, and both are actionable in a way that "total events" is not.

Book ch. 8 & 9 · Clustering

Groups by working style, not by how much

Clustering raw activity counts produces one finding: some students do more than others. To get past that, these ten features are all rates, ratios or shapes — pass rate, errors per run, runs per edit, median session length, share of evening and weekend work, day-to-day variability, weekday spread, events per active day, exercises solved per hour. Standardised, then k-means at k=4.

The four profiles differ on when and how students work rather than how much. They do not separate clearly on exercises passed: medians run from 95 to 122 (Kruskal–Wallis H = 5.56, p = 0.14). Mean self-efficacy runs from 4.04 to 5.51 on the seven-point scale.

The group that stands out is the evening irregulars: 31% of its activity in the evening, 15% at weekends, the most irregular day-to-day rhythm, the longest total hours — and the highest self-efficacy of the four. The contrast with the long-session regulars is the interesting one. They work in the longest sessions (median 50 minutes), mostly inside school hours, very regularly, and report the lowest self-efficacy. Same institution, similar exercise counts, opposite confidence.

Report the silhouette honestly. Silhouette width peaks at k=2 (0.23) and sits at 0.15 for k=4. These are weak separations: the profiles describe how students work, but they do not separate clearly on outcomes, and the clusters are not discrete types. Model-based clustering (book ch. 9) puts a likelihood on that question instead of leaving it to a heuristic index. Chapter 5 takes that step: the students form one continuous population, so read these four styles as regions of it, not as types.
Book ch. 10 & 11 · Sequence analysis & VaSSTra

Engagement as a sequence of weekly states

Each student-week is coded into one of four states by event volume, using tertiles of the non-zero distribution as cuts: Absent (no events), Low (<232), Moderate (232–479), Intense (>479). That turns 153 students into 153 sequences of 36 symbols — the VaSSTra move of going from variables to states to trajectories.

The shape is a steady drain from the top state. In week 0, 113 of 153 students are Intense and none are Absent. By week 20, that is 21 Intense and 91 Low. Students are not dropping out — Absent stays low outside holidays — they are settling into a lower gear.

Every state is self-sustaining: the diagonal is the largest entry in each row (0.38 to 0.47). The asymmetry is at the edges. From Intense, the chance of being Absent next week is 0.06; from Absent, the chance of returning to Intense is 0.09 while the chance of staying Absent is 0.41. Disengagement is stickier than engagement, which is the quantitative case for intervening on a first missed week rather than a pattern of them.

In the story, the lower gear is a stall, and a first missed week is where a drop begins.

Book ch. 12 & 14 · Markov models & process mining

The edit–run–error loop, one step at a time

Zooming from weeks to seconds. Within a session, what follows what? Rows are the current event, columns the next event, restricted to the seven event types that carry the work.

Three rows tell most of the story.

  • After a failed submission, the most likely next logged action is a run (0.70), not an edit (0.20).
  • After a compile error, the same pattern: run 0.59, edit 0.30. But edits are logged coarsely. After a syntax error, a next run with no logged edit in between comes back clean 35% of the time, which unchanged code cannot do, so many of these runs follow edits the log does not record. Timing the same logs gives a cleaner measure: a rerun within 3 seconds, too fast for an edit, follows 15% of syntax errors and shows the same message again 91% of the time.
  • After a correct submission, 0.84 go straight to a pageview — they move on immediately. Almost nobody revisits working code.

This is the kind of finding that only a transition model surfaces. Aggregate counts show that students make errors; the transition matrix shows what they do about them: mostly run again, often after an edit the log does not show. Process mining (book ch. 14) would turn this into a discovered model with the loops made explicit; mixture Markov models (book ch. 12) would ask whether the run-first pattern belongs to a subgroup rather than to everyone.

Story thread · Stalls

A quick rerun of the same error is a reflex rather than a strategy, and a run that shows the same message again is practice without a new mastery experience: a stall, in the story’s terms. Book ch. 19–21 · Psychological networks, factor analysis, SEM

Twenty items, one strong factor and a telling second

The instrument holds up: Cronbach's α = 0.954 across 20 items, mean inter-item r = 0.54, first eigenvalue 11.35 of 20. Students do discriminate between topics rather than flat-lining — the median within-student spread across items is 1.19 scale points.

The first component is general programming self-efficacy, loading 0.59–0.86 on everything. The second (eigenvalue 2.55) is bipolar and unambiguous: tracing and control-flow items load positively (+0.03 to +0.37), while every object-orientation and recursion item loads negatively (−0.49 to −0.70). The instrument is measuring a basics dimension and an advanced-topics dimension, and that is a factor structure worth reporting rather than averaging away.

The two data sources agree on where the course gets hard. The five lowest-rated items are inheritance, class-vs-instance, recursive patterns, overload resolution and tracing recursion. Independently, the log data puts the lowest exercise pass rates in units 8 and 9 of the course — 9.2.2 Flying letters at 4.1% across 663 attempts, 8.2.2 Sunny meadow at 9.5%, 9.3.2 Character rain at 10.0% — and only 49 of the 153 students pass anything in unit 11. Self-report and behaviour locate the same wall. Students with higher self-efficacy push on there: they attempt more of units 8–10 (ρ .34) and try longer before leaving an exercise unsolved (.37), but they do not clearly solve more of the exercises they struggle with (.16; an exploratory check, Chapter 15).

Item non-response points the same way. The recursion and OOP items have 10–14 missing answers each against 0–6 elsewhere: students who never reached that material declined to rate themselves on it. That missingness is informative, not random, and listwise deletion would quietly drop the students the finding is about.

Triadic reciprocal causation

Where behaviour and self-efficacy actually meet

Linking the two halves directly: 96 students with both a survey response and log data. A ridge regression predicting mean self-efficacy from twelve behavioural features reaches a cross-validated R² of 0.15 (averaged over 50 five-fold splits). Behaviour explains about a seventh of the variance in confidence — real, and modest. Chapter 2 reaches R² .18–.22 in its key windows with 70 log features (raw or as family factor scores), random forests and the 15-item core score as the outcome, against twelve features, a ridge regression and the 20-item mean here; both say the logs carry a modest part of self-efficacy.

Volume and rhythm both count. Total hours is the strongest correlate (ρ = +0.35), with active days (+0.33) close behind. Three others are about how students work:

  • Day-to-day variability goes with higher self-efficacy (ρ = +0.32). Bursty workers report higher self-efficacy than metronomic ones.
  • Median session length runs the other way (ρ = −0.28). Students who sit in long sessions report lower confidence — consistent with long sessions being a symptom of struggle rather than a sign of investment.
  • Errors per run is negative (ρ = −0.26), while raw error counts are not significant (ρ = +0.13, p = 0.20). The rate matters; the volume does not.

Submission pass rate is flatly unrelated to self-efficacy (ρ = +0.03, p = 0.81). That is the result most in tension with a simple mastery-experience account, where success should feed confidence most directly. The likely confound is that pass rate is driven by which exercises a student chose to attempt, so it mixes difficulty selection with ability. Controlling for exercise difficulty — the catalogue carries points — is the test that would separate them.

Story thread · Improves

The test proposed here comes close to being run in Chapter 2: the completion rate of attempted exercises goes with self-efficacy (ρ .46 by points), and so does recovering from an error on the next run (ρ .42). What goes with confidence is finishing, not first-time success.

Read through the triadic frame, the environmental vertex is doing visible work here: cohort timetables set the weekday and hour signatures, the course calendar produces the three collapsed weeks, and the unit-8 wall is a property of the curriculum rather than of any student. A model that treats behavioural features as purely personal will attribute curriculum effects to students.
