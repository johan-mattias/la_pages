Source material

Six files, one join key, four traps

Everything hangs off cs_anon.csv, the bridge between the platform's numeric user_id and the survey's SCT-… identifier. 102 of 103 survey respondents link to an account with log data.

Four joins that fail silently.
  • cs_anon.user_id contains the literal string na, so pandas types the column as text and an unconverted join to the logs returns zero matches with no error.
  • Four students appear twice in cs_anon.csv — once labelled with a survey id and class, once as na. Merging on user_id fans their events out into duplicate rows. Keep the labelled row before joining.
  • The survey files disagree on headers: group  with a trailing space and start year in TE22, group/start_year in TE23. The TE23 file also lost the header for the class/instance item, which arrives as Unnamed: 20.
  • The TE22 log calls the unix-time column extract; the TE23 log calls it unix_time. Same content.

The two log files do not share event ids. 40 students appear in both because they continued across course instances, but their events are distinct, so a concatenation is safe once cs_anon is deduplicated.

The largest threat to validity is 57 students. They carry the group label na, have no survey response, and are invisible to every linked analysis — yet their median activity (14,500 events) matches the labelled classes. That is 36% of the logged population excluded from any behaviour-to-self-efficacy claim. Establishing whether this is a consent boundary or a data-entry gap changes what the survey half can support.

Chapter 7 · Predictive modeling

How early can you see it coming?

The question an early-warning system has to answer: using only what a student did in their first n days, can you tell who ends the year in the upper half for exercises passed? Features are computed inside the window only; the outcome is the full-year count. Five-fold stratified cross-validation, 159 students, base rate 49.7%.

Two weeks of data already reach AUC 0.83 with a linear model. The curve then climbs slowly — the extra ten weeks between day 14 and day 84 buy about 0.08 AUC. For an intervention that has to happen while there is still time to act, the information is almost all present by the end of the first month.

Watch the target definition. An earlier run of this model used whole-course features to predict a whole-course outcome and scored AUC 0.92 — almost entirely because the feature submissions and the target exercises passed are the same construct measured twice. Restricting features to an early window is what makes the number mean something. Leakage in log data usually looks like a feature that is a component of the outcome rather than a cause of it.

Two process features survive alongside the volume measures. Errors per run correlates −0.35 with final exercises passed: students who trigger a compile or runtime error on a larger share of their executions finish behind. Runs per edit is similar at −0.29 — running repeatedly without editing in between is the behavioural signature of guessing. Neither is a volume measure, and both are actionable in a way that "total events" is not.

Chapters 8 & 9 · Clustering

Groups by working style, not by how much

Clustering raw activity counts produces one finding: some students do more than others. To get past that, these ten features are all rates, ratios or shapes — pass rate, errors per run, runs per edit, median session length, share of evening and weekend work, day-to-day variability, weekday spread, events per active day, exercises solved per hour. Standardised, then k-means at k=4.

The four profiles differ on when and how students work rather than how much, and they separate on outcomes: median exercises passed runs from 92 to 122 (Kruskal–Wallis H = 10.87, p = 0.012), and mean self-efficacy from 4.22 to 5.36 on the seven-point scale.

The group that stands out is cluster 2: 30% of its activity in the evening, 15% at weekends, the most irregular day-to-day rhythm, the longest total hours — and the highest self-efficacy of the four. The contrast with cluster 0 is the interesting one. Cluster 0 works in the longest sessions (median 49 minutes), almost entirely inside school hours, very regularly, and reports the lowest self-efficacy. Same institution, similar exercise counts, opposite confidence.

Report the silhouette honestly. Silhouette width peaks at k=2 (0.23) and sits at 0.15 for k=4. These are weak-to-moderate separations — the structure is real enough to predict outcomes but the clusters are not discrete types. Chapter 9's model-based approach would put a likelihood on that question instead of leaving it to a heuristic index, and is the natural next step before committing to a four-group story in a paper.
Chapters 10 & 11 · Sequence analysis & VaSSTra

Engagement as a sequence of weekly states

Each student-week is coded into one of four states by event volume, using tertiles of the non-zero distribution as cuts: Absent (no events), Low (<229), Moderate (229–473), Intense (>473). That turns 159 students into 159 sequences of 36 symbols — the VaSSTra move of going from variables to states to trajectories.

The shape is a steady drain from the top state. In week 0, 119 of 159 students are Intense and none are Absent. By week 20, that is 23 Intense and 92 Low. Students are not dropping out — Absent stays low outside holidays — they are settling into a lower gear.

Every state is self-sustaining: the diagonal is the largest entry in each row (0.41 to 0.48). The asymmetry is at the edges. From Intense, the chance of being Absent next week is 0.06; from Absent, the chance of returning to Intense is 0.09 while the chance of staying Absent is 0.41. Disengagement is stickier than engagement, which is the quantitative case for intervening on a first missed week rather than a pattern of them.

Chapters 12 & 14 · Markov models & process mining

The edit–run–error loop, one step at a time

Zooming from weeks to seconds. Within a session, what follows what? Rows are the current event, columns the next event, restricted to the seven event types that carry the work.

Three rows tell most of the story.

  • After a failed submission, the most likely next action is re-running the code (0.69) — not editing it (0.20). Students re-execute before they revise.
  • After a compile error, the same pattern, harder: run again 0.60, edit 0.30. Running the identical program after a compile error cannot change the result, so this is a genuine misconception about what the run button does, visible 21,000 times.
  • After a correct submission, 0.85 go straight to a pageview — they move on immediately. Almost nobody revisits working code.

This is the kind of finding that only a transition model surfaces. Aggregate counts show that students make errors; the transition matrix shows what they do about them, and the answer is "press run again". Chapter 14's process mining would turn this into a discovered model with the loops made explicit; chapter 12's mixture Markov models would ask whether the run-first pattern belongs to a subgroup rather than to everyone.

Chapters 19–21 · Psychological networks, factor analysis, SEM

Twenty items, one strong factor and a telling second

The instrument holds up: Cronbach's α = 0.958 across 20 items, mean inter-item r = 0.56, first eigenvalue 11.74 of 20. Students do discriminate between topics rather than flat-lining — the median within-student spread across items is 1.15 scale points.

The first component is general programming self-efficacy, loading 0.61–0.88 on everything. The second (eigenvalue 2.44) is bipolar and unambiguous: tracing and control-flow items load positively (+0.08 to +0.35), while every object-orientation and recursion item loads negatively (−0.49 to −0.68). The instrument is measuring a basics dimension and an advanced-topics dimension, and that is a factor structure worth reporting rather than averaging away.

The two data sources agree on where the course gets hard. The five lowest-rated items are inheritance, recursive patterns, class-vs-instance, overload resolution and tracing recursion. Independently, the log data puts the lowest exercise pass rates in chapters 8 and 9 of cs1-22 — 9.2.2 Flying letters at 4.2% across 665 attempts, 8.2.2 Sunny meadow at 9.6%, 9.3.2 Character rain at 9.8% — and most students never pass anything in chapter 10. Self-report and behaviour locate the same wall.

Item non-response points the same way. The recursion and OOP items have 11–15 missing answers each against 0–6 elsewhere: students who never reached that material declined to rate themselves on it. That missingness is informative, not random, and listwise deletion would quietly drop the students the finding is about.

Triadic reciprocal causation

Where behaviour and self-efficacy actually meet

Linking the two halves directly: 102 students with both a survey response and log data. A ridge regression predicting mean self-efficacy from twelve behavioural features reaches a cross-validated R² of 0.10. Behaviour explains a tenth of the variance in confidence — real, and modest.

The pattern is not "students who do more feel more capable". Three of the strongest associations are about rhythm:

  • Day-to-day variability is the single strongest correlate (ρ = +0.33). Bursty workers report higher self-efficacy than metronomic ones.
  • Median session length runs the other way (ρ = −0.28). Students who sit in long sessions report lower confidence — consistent with long sessions being a symptom of struggle rather than a sign of investment.
  • Errors per run is negative (ρ = −0.22), while raw error counts are not significant. The rate matters; the volume does not.

Submission pass rate is flatly unrelated to self-efficacy (ρ = +0.04, p = 0.67). That is the result most in tension with a simple mastery-experience account, where success should feed confidence most directly. The likely confound is that pass rate is driven by which exercises a student chose to attempt, so it mixes difficulty selection with ability. Controlling for exercise difficulty — the catalogue carries points — is the test that would separate them.

Read through the triadic frame, the environmental vertex is doing visible work here: cohort timetables set the weekday and hour signatures, the course calendar produces the three collapsed weeks, and the chapter-8 wall is a property of the curriculum rather than of any student. A model that treats behavioural features as purely personal will attribute curriculum effects to students.
