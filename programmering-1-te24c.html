Programmering 1 te24c

How the 32 students in te24c worked through the course: which exercises they chose, how long they took, how fast they moved, how they handled errors, what they built, how they read the lesson pages and who was elsewhere during lessons. Grades come in at the end, and the behaviour measures are then combined into student groups.

The event log ends 31 Jan 2026Errors, lesson-page reading and window focus cover units 1–4 only (59% of all completions). Points, time on task, pace and code cover the whole course.
Self-efficacy covers 22 studentsThe codebook links 26 students to their survey codes; 22 answered both the June 2025 and the August 2026 survey. Five students have no code.
The groups are exploratoryWith 32 students the behaviour groups are weak (silhouette 0.13). Read them as typical combinations; the student table at the end lets you check each case.

01Data and coverage

32 students, 31 of them with grades (purpleSeal9009 has platform data but no grade). The platform export gives a score, a completion time and minutes on task for 583 items; the code zip holds every saved program; the event log has 75,859 events for this class. Log accounts were matched to usernames from completion timestamps, and eu-student-usernames.csv confirms all 32.

The class had two lessons in November, worked on a group creative task in December, and did most of the course between 13 January and 8 April. April and May went to the final project. Nobody got past a few unit 8 exercises, so units 9–11 were not reached.

  • Platform time on task matches the active time reconstructed from the log (per-item Spearman ρ 0.91–0.96 in units 1–4; totals within 4–6% for units 1, 2 and 4). It counts time with the window in focus, so it is used as the time measure for the whole course.
  • Two accounts that appear from 20–21 January (grayBird4387, purpleHorse2171) are not in the class lists and are left out.

02Points and difficulty

Most of the work was 1-point exercises: 633 of 891 attempted (71%) and 552 of 765 solved (72%). 3-point exercises were solved 69% of the times they were tried, against 87–88% for 1- and 2-point exercises.

  • Harder-exercise oriented (at least a third of attempts at 2–3 points, 6 students). blueSeal8903, orangeBadger3196 and purpleSnail8876 did almost every exercise in each set. pinkKoala5151 and orangeBee4594 covered more of a set's 2–3-point exercises than its 1-point ones, so they skipped easy items to reach harder ones.
  • 1-point focused (under 15%, 7 students). Sets run from 1 to 3 points, so this mostly means stopping after the first exercises in a set rather than skipping around.
  • Low activity (fewer than 10 exercises in the whole course, 8 students). Several of them did put time into creative tasks and the final project, which shows in the time panel.
  • 10 of the 15 stayed at about the same level, 2 moved to harder work (purpleZebra6522 from 8% to 56%, purpleDolphin0371 from 21% to 42%) and 3 moved to easier work (orangeBee4594, redJellyfish2703, blackHamster7494). For the other 17 there is too little work after unit 2 to tell.
  • Unit 3 is a special case for the whole class: 60 of 62 attempts were 1-point exercises, mostly the first exercise of each set.

03Time on task and difficulty

Harder exercises took longer, and later units took longer at every point level. For the same student, a 2-point exercise took 1.2× and a 3-point exercise 2.7× as long as a 1-point one (mixed model on log minutes, solved exercises).

  • Holding point value constant, exercises took 1.5× (unit 2) to 2.8× (unit 3) as long as in unit 1. Unit 3 (onMouseMove/Drag, first if statements) and unit 6 (groups, onStep, motion) were the slowest; unit 7 (for loops) was not slower than units 4–5.
  • Your two halves of the course: exercises in units 7–8 took 1.1× the time of same-value exercises in units 1–6, which is not a reliable difference (p = .27). Only 17 students reached unit 7 and they were mostly the stronger ones, so the loops half was not harder for those who got there.
  • Across students, more time went with more points (ρ = 0.89 over the course). Unit 3 is the exception (ρ = 0.20): nearly everyone did the same few 1-point exercises, so time there mostly shows how hard those exercises were for each student.

04Pace

Pace was mostly fixed early. 11 students were ahead of the class median on at least two thirds of lesson days and 12 were behind; 9 moved around the middle. 92% of all completions happened during lessons, so pace mostly reflects how much each student got done in class.

05Errors and learning to fix them

In units 1–4 a quarter of runs (24%) ended in an error: 10% syntax and 14% runtime. A typical error was gone within 15 seconds. With practice, students got faster at some syntax errors, but not at runtime and graphics-library errors. TE22 and TE23 showed the same pattern.

  • Error share by unit: 23% of runs in unit 1, 25% in unit 2 and 27% in unit 3, where syntax errors peak at 13% with the first if statements. Unit 4 shows 20%, but only 9 students reached it before the log ends.
  • The vague SyntaxError: invalid syntax got 35% faster per doubling of encounters (95% CI −54% to −9%). Missing colon, indentation block and = for == also trend faster (−9% to −21%), but their intervals cross zero. A missing : became a one-try fix more often: the odds rose 1.9× per doubling (p = .007).
  • NameError (0%) and shape-argument errors (−1%) did not change. Shape property values got 12% slower (95% CI 3–22%), probably because programs grew; this disappears when unit is held constant.
  • 69% of debugging episodes involved a single error message; 10% went through three or more different messages before the code ran cleanly.
  • The December group task is kept apart: 32% of runs there had errors, and several students edited the same program, so a fix can't be credited to one person.
  • Fastest fixers relative to the error type: orangeBee4594, orangeElephant3269, redBear4041, redJellyfish2703. Slowest: blackLion0079, orangeOwl9334, graySeal3799, redDog7989.

06Exercise types and persistence

Students did the mandatory checkpoints and picked from the rest. From unit 2 on, a median of 26 of 32 students completed each checkpoint, and 97% of checkpoint attempts were solved. Of the optional exercises in the units each student reached, 13% were attempted and 86% of those attempts were solved; the end-of-unit and challenge sets were hardly touched (3%).

  • When. Checkpoints and the optional section exercises were done in the same lessons as the lesson pages (median about a day before the student finished the unit's pages). End-of-unit exercises came afterwards, a median of 4 days after the student's last lesson page in the unit. 93–100% were solved during lessons.
  • A narrower choice later on. In units 5–7 only 18–45% of the optional exercises were opened by anyone, against all of them in units 2–4 (78% in unit 1), so in the later units the class seems to have worked from a smaller selection.
  • Struggle rarely meant quitting. In the logged units, 310 attempts involved real struggle (2+ failed submissions or 3+ runs with errors). 90% of them still ended solved, against 93% of attempts without struggle.
  • Difficulty drives giving up. Students left 15% of engaged 1- and 2-point exercises unsolved, 31% of 3-point exercises and 4 of 6 challenge exercises.
  • Profiles. Quick solvers (12) are at or below classmates' time and leave under 20% unsolved. Persisters (7) are slower but still finish, and four of them are 1-point focused. Quick or moves on (3: orangeElephant3269, redSalmon0866, redBear4041) solve fast when they solve but leave a fifth or more. Struggles and moves on (7) are slower and leave a fifth or more.
  • The profiles use all exercises, checkpoints included. Counting only the optional exercises gives almost the same give-up measure (ρ = 0.98), but students close to the cut-offs can switch label; blackLion0079, for example, sits on the line between persister and quick solver.

07Creative-task code

Programs grew in both length and decisions over the course. For the 22 students with both a Creative Task 3 program and an individual final project, median length went from 39 to 112 lines and decisions (if/elif, for/while, and/or) from 7 to 28.5. Loops arrive late: 22% of Creative Task 3 programs use a for loop, against 77% of final projects.

  • "Creative Task 3" is the task handed in 3–11 February. Students used the creative-task slot of the unit they were in: 3.5.2 (17), 4.5 (7) or 2.5 (3).
  • Decisions track the teacher's grade better than length does: Creative Task 3 decisions vs its grade ρ = 0.65 (lines 0.54); final-project decisions vs final grade ρ = 0.74 (lines 0.59, number of course units' concepts used 0.63, minutes 0.48).
  • No copying between students: the largest 5-token overlap between programs by different students is 0.19, between two 4-line stubs. One program was a pasted example (purpleSnail3941's 8.5 Creative task 1 is the Ghost attack example) and is excluded.
  • Two final projects are far longer than the rest (redDeer7431 with 1,108 lines and 70 functions, orangeElephant3269 with 643). All correlations are rank-based, so these two don't drive them. orangeBee4594's final project had lost its indentation in the export, so its decisions were counted from keywords.

08Lesson pages

A student spent a median of about 3 minutes on a lesson page (3.4 in unit 2, 3.0 in unit 3, 2.6 in unit 4; unit 1 pages have no questions and got about a minute). In 24% of student–page pairs the student came back on a later day. First answers to the multiple-choice questions were right 67% of the time.

  • A habit, not a moment. Within the same student, quick and slow answers were equally often right (odds ratio 1.02 per doubling of reading time, 95% CI 0.82–1.27). Between students, those who generally read longer were right on the first try more often (odds ratio 1.57 per doubling, p = .002).
  • The lock-out. After the 2nd wrong answer (and again after the 4th and 6th) the next answer never came sooner than 10 s, with a median of 15 s. After the 1st, 3rd or 5th wrong answer students tried again within 2–10 s. So in this log the timeout follows every second wrong answer and lasts about 10 s, not 15 s after the third. The platform may have changed since you checked.
  • Most lock-outs: blueSeal8903 (17), orangeOwl9334 (14), whiteElephant5373 (10), redCamel8040 (8). blueSeal8903 is a strong student who guesses quickly on the questions; the other three combine guessing with low first-try accuracy.
  • First-try accuracy does not predict the final grade (ρ = 0.19). Coming back to lesson pages goes with lower grades (ρ = −0.46), probably students looking for help.
  • Reading time counts active time on the page, excluding time in other windows. Gaps up to 5 minutes count as reading, because reading produces no events.

09Distraction: window focus and blur

Raw blur and focus counts don't single out orangeOwl9334, blackLion0079, redBeaver3376 and whiteElephant5373. Time away from CS Academy during lessons does: in the lessons they attended they were away for 46% of the time, against 25% for their classmates.

  • Why blur counts mislead. 40% of blur→focus pairs happen within one second and land on another CS Academy page, mostly switching between an exercise and the docs. The four had 23 blur events per lesson hour against 19 for the others (p = .72).
  • What does differ. Absences of a minute or more: 5.0 against 2.8 per lesson hour (p = .003). Lesson minutes with any activity: 40% against 55% (p = .04). They were away more in every one of the 11 lessons, most clearly in November and late January.
  • They rank 1st (redBeaver3376, 58%), 3rd (whiteElephant5373), 5th (orangeOwl9334) and 6th (blackLion0079) in time away. The other two in the top six are redDeer7431, a strong student present in only 6 of the 11 lessons whose large programs suggest work outside the platform, and greenHorse2592.
  • What else they share. All four got E on Creative Task 3 and D or E as final grades, worked mostly on 1-point exercises or very few exercises, were behind or at the median in pace, and three of them hit the question lock-out far more often than most (14, 10 and 7 times; class median 3). They also worked together: two pairs in the December group task and one group of four in the semester project.
  • Away does not always mean gaming. It also covers listening to the teacher, using a desktop editor (the class opened the desktop CMU Graphics docs on 25 November) or reading elsewhere.

10Grades and self-efficacy

The behaviour measures line up with the grades in a consistent way: what students built and how efficiently they worked mattered more than how long they spent or how often they switched windows.

  • Strongest: final-project decisions (ρ = 0.74), minutes per point (−0.69), quick solves (0.62) and final-project concept breadth (0.63). Time away (−0.44) and long absences (−0.48) are clearly negative.
  • Unrelated: raw blur events (−0.06), first-try accuracy on lesson questions (0.19), lessons attended in the log period (−0.15) and failed submissions per solve (−0.09).
  • Pace groups (E = 1 … A = 5): usually ahead averages 4.45, around the median 3.44 and usually behind 2.73; Kruskal–Wallis p = .009.
  • With 31 students the intervals are about ±0.3 wide, so read these as an ordering rather than precise effect sizes.

Self-efficacy, student by student

With the codebook, 26 of the 31 graded students have a survey code. 24 answered the June 2025 survey (before the course) and 24 the August 2026 survey (after it); 22 answered both. For those 22 the mean rose from 4.45 to 5.06 on the 7-point scale (Wilcoxon p = .004), and 17 of 22 went up. te24c started higher than te24a (3.8) and te24b (2.9) and gained less (+0.6 against +1.3 and +1.6).

  • The June value already predicted the grade. Self-efficacy five months before the course correlates ρ = 0.56 with the final grade. Students who started confident were also more efficient (minutes per point ρ = −0.48), but they did not do more exercises (−0.11) or work further ahead (0.00).
  • Mastery goes with growth. Students who earned more exercise points gained more (ρ = 0.49), and so, less clearly, did those who worked further ahead (0.43). Holding the June value constant, the share of quick solves still adds to the August value (p = .03). This fits Bandura's mastery experiences as the main source of self-efficacy.
  • After the course it tracks performance closely: ρ = 0.81 with the final grade, 0.71 with the Creative Task 3 grade, 0.74 with the decisions in the Creative Task 3 program and −0.63 with minutes per point. The August survey came after the grades were out, so the grade itself is part of what students rated (holding June constant, adding the final grade raises R² from 0.60 to 0.81).
  • Groups and pace. August self-efficacy differs between the behaviour groups (Kruskal–Wallis p = .045): efficient builders 6.0, careful readers 5.6, front-runners 5.2, slow and low volume 4.0 and distracted, guessing 3.1 (medians). Students who were usually ahead gained most (median +0.9) and those usually behind least (+0.1; p = .046).
  • Persistence. The two coping items, "trace correctly after earlier failures" and "write working code after hours of bugs", go with finishing and quick solving in August (failures item vs exercises left unsolved ρ = −0.51, vs quick solves 0.70), but not with how often students pushed through a logged struggle. The slower-but-finishing persisters rate themselves low (median 3.8 in August), so finishing despite difficulty did not turn into feeling capable.
  • Calibration. Ratings match grades well overall. Two front-runners rate themselves lower than their A suggests (orangeBadger3196 5.1, purpleSnail8876 5.2), while pinkKoala5151 (C) and blueSeal8903 (B) rate themselves higher than their grades. redCamel8040 made the largest jump (1.1 → 4.3) and redPenguin2134 the largest drop (6.0 → 4.2).
  • The observed gaming students rate themselves low in August: 3.1, 3.0 and 3.8 (blackLion0079 did not answer), against a median of 5.5 for the rest (p = .02).
  • AI self-efficacy (five new items in August) correlates 0.64 with the final grade, mostly through general self-efficacy (0.27 when August self-efficacy is held constant).
  • Not linked: five students have no code (orangeSwan9877, redDog7989, orangeBee4594, purpleZebra6522, pinkPanda6464), and four August answers labelled te24c carry codes that are not in the codebook; they may belong to those five. purpleSnail3941's August answer is labelled te24b but matches by code.
  • Largest gains: designing tests (+1.1), tracing nested structures (+1.0), writing and calling functions (+0.95), constructing a boolean expression (+0.9) and tracing after earlier failures (+0.8). Smallest: decomposing a problem (+0.2) and tracing with the same variable names (+0.3).

11Groups and trajectories

Combining 11 behaviour measures, with grades left out, gives six groups. The structure is weak (silhouette 0.13; bootstrap stability 0.53–0.73), so read them as typical combinations rather than sharp types. Final grades differ clearly between them (Kruskal–Wallis p = .007).

  • Behaviours that go together. Harder exercises, being ahead and quick solving travel together: all 6 harder-exercise students were usually ahead and 5 of them are quick solvers. 1-point focus goes with slower but finishing (4 of 7). Low activity goes with being behind (7 of 8).
  • Trajectories. Front-runners pull away in mid-January and stay 4–10 items ahead. Efficient builders and careful readers stay just above the median. Slow and low-volume students drift steadily down. The distracted group stays a little below the median, and the early leavers drop away once they stop logging in.
  • August self-efficacy also differs between the groups (Kruskal–Wallis p = .045), from a median of 6.0 for efficient builders to 3.1 for the distracted group, even though self-efficacy was not used to form them.
  • Ward clustering on rank-normal scores; k = 4 agrees with k-means (adjusted Rand 0.80), and k = 6 splits its two large groups. Missing values (6 students had too few error spells) were set to the class median.

13Method notes

  • Files. The grades-and-time export was split into its three tables (scores, completion times, minutes). gradebook9 and progress9 repeat the same scores (progress fills never-opened items with 0) and were only used to name exercise sets. Point values come from exercise_info_eu.csv (course cs1-22) and match every observed score.
  • Exercise types. Checkpoints are the only mandatory exercises; all other exercises are optional (confirmed by the teacher).
  • Item status. Solved = full points; attempted = score 0 (submitted, never passed); opened = time on task but no score. Lesson pages have a constant score of 2 and count as done when they have a completion time.
  • Pace. 21 class days (10+ students completing something). Usually ahead or behind = above or below the class median on at least two thirds of the days from 13 January.
  • Errors. Same definitions as the TE22/TE23 error study: a spell follows one exact message on one page until a run without it; fresh = started by the student's own run of code they had edited; learning slope = log fix time on log2 of the k-th encounter with student fixed effects and student-clustered standard errors.
  • Lessons in the log. Minutes where at least a third of the class was active within ±4 minutes, with a peak of two thirds; 11 lessons between 18 November and 28 January.
  • Code. Static analysis of the zip (programs are parsed, never run). Decisions = if + elif + for + while + and/or operators. Programs under 10 lines or mostly copied from an example are excluded.
  • Self-efficacy. Codebook_-_te24c.csv links survey codes to usernames. A survey counts when at least 10 of the 15 items were answered (graySeal3799's June answer was empty and redBeaver3376 answered 5 items). Mean of the 15 common items; the five AI items are kept separate.
  • Small numbers. 31 graded students: every correlation has a wide interval and the groups are exploratory. The log-based parts cover units 1–4 only.
Scripts and a findings summary are saved in the project under claude/te24c/.
