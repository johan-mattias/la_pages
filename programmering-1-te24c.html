One class, surveyed before and after

How the 27 students in te24c whose platform data may be used worked through the course: which exercises they chose, how long they took, how fast they moved, how they handled errors, what they built, how they read the lesson pages and how much lesson time they spent away from the platform. Grades come in at the end. The page shows the class and groups of students, never single students.

The event log ends 31 Jan 2026Errors, lesson-page reading and window focus cover units 1–4 only (59% of all completions). Points, time on task, pace and code cover the whole course.
Self-efficacy before and after17 students answered both the June 2025 survey, before the course, and the August 2026 survey, after it.
Small groupsGrades and self-efficacy are summarised only for groups of five or more students.
ConsentFive students who declined the use of their CS Academy data are left out, and the grades of eight who declined StudyBee data are not used. Survey answers count only with a “Ja” to the survey's own consent question.
Many comparisonsThe page reports dozens of correlations and group comparisons for one class of 18–27 students, without correction for multiple testing. Read single results as indications, and the intervals as wide.

01Data and coverage

27 students, 18 of them with grades. The platform export gives a score, a completion time and minutes on task for 583 items; the code export holds every saved program; the event log has 63,435 events for this class. Log accounts were matched to the class list from completion timestamps, and the platform's account list confirms all 27.

The class had two lessons in November, worked on a group creative task in December, and did most of the course between 13 January and 8 April. April and May went to the final project. Nobody got past a few unit 8 exercises, so units 9–11 were not reached.

  • Platform time on task matches the active time reconstructed from the log (per-item Spearman ρ 0.91–0.96 in units 1–4; totals within 6% for units 1, 2 and 4). It counts time with the window in focus, so it is used as the time measure for the whole course.
  • Two accounts that appear from 20–21 January are not in the class lists and are left out.

02Points and difficulty

Most of the work was 1-point exercises: 554 of 801 attempted (69%) and 491 of 696 solved (71%). 3-point exercises were solved 69% of the times they were tried, against 89% for 1- and 2-point exercises.

  • Harder-exercise oriented (at least a third of attempts at 2–3 points, 6 students). Two of them covered more of a set's 2–3-point exercises than its 1-point ones, so they skipped easy items to reach harder ones.
  • 1-point focused (under 15%, 5 students). Sets run from 1 to 3 points, so this mostly means stopping after the first exercises in a set rather than skipping around.
  • Low activity (fewer than 10 exercises in the whole course, 7 students). Several of them did put time into creative tasks and the final project, which shows in the time panel.
  • Did students change difficulty? Of the 14 students with at least 5 attempts in two course phases (units 1–2, 3–5, 6–8), 9 stayed at about the same share of harder exercises, 2 moved to harder work and 3 to easier work. For the other 13 there is too little work after unit 2 to tell.
  • Unit 3 is a special case for the whole class: 52 of 54 attempts were 1-point exercises, mostly the first exercise of each set.

03Time on task and difficulty

Harder exercises took longer, and later units took longer at every point level. For the same student, a 2-point exercise took 1.3× and a 3-point exercise 2.8× as long as a 1-point one (mixed model on log minutes, solved exercises).

  • Holding point value constant, exercises took 1.5× (unit 2) to 2.7× (unit 3) as long as in unit 1. Unit 3 (onMouseMove/Drag, first if statements) and unit 6 (groups, onStep, motion) were the slowest; unit 7 (for loops) was about as slow as units 4–5 (1.8× against 1.8× and 2.0×).
  • The two halves of the course: exercises in units 7–8 took 1.2× the time of same-value exercises in units 1–6, which is not a reliable difference (p = .12). Only 15 students reached unit 7 and they were mostly the stronger ones, so the loops half was not harder for those who got there.
  • Across students, more time went with more points (ρ = 0.87 over the course). Unit 3 is the exception (ρ = 0.07): nearly everyone did the same few 1-point exercises, so time there mostly shows how hard those exercises were for each student.

04Pace

Pace was mostly fixed early. 11 students were ahead of the class median on at least two thirds of lesson days and 10 were behind; 6 moved around the middle. 93% of all completions happened during lessons, so pace mostly reflects how much each student got done in class.

  • Trajectories. 8 of the 10 students who were usually behind fell further behind after February (lead in the second half at least 3 items lower than in the first). Two students from the middle caught up.
  • Pace goes with difficulty: all 6 harder-exercise students were usually ahead, and 6 of the 7 low-activity students were usually behind.

05Errors and learning to fix them

In units 1–4 a quarter of runs (23%) ended in an error: 10% syntax and 14% runtime. A typical error was gone within 15 seconds. With practice, students got faster at some syntax errors, but not at runtime and graphics-library errors.

  • Error share by unit: 24% of runs in unit 1, 24% in unit 2 and 25% in unit 3, where syntax errors peak at 11% with the first if statements. Unit 4 shows 20%, but only 9 students reached it before the log ends.
  • The vague SyntaxError: invalid syntax got 33% faster per doubling of encounters (95% CI −52% to −5%), unlike in TE22 and TE23 (Chapter 10). Missing colon, indentation block and = for == also trend faster (−3% to −11%), but their intervals cross zero.
  • NameError (0%) and shape-argument errors (−3%) did not change. Shape property values got 13% slower (95% CI 2–25%), probably because programs grew; this disappears when unit is held constant.
  • 69% of debugging episodes involved a single error message; 10% went through three or more different messages before the code ran cleanly.
  • The December group task is kept apart: 28% of runs there had errors, and several students edited the same program, so a fix can't be credited to one person.

06Exercise types and persistence

Students did the mandatory checkpoints and picked from the rest. From unit 2 on, a median of 22 of 27 students completed each checkpoint, and 97% of checkpoint attempts were solved. Of the optional exercises in the units each student reached, 14% were attempted and 87% of those attempts were solved; the end-of-unit and challenge sets were hardly touched (4%).

  • When. Checkpoints and the optional section exercises were done in the same lessons as the lesson pages (median about a day before the student finished the unit's pages). End-of-unit exercises came afterwards, a median of 4 days after the student's last lesson page in the unit. 94–100% were solved during lessons.
  • A narrower choice later on. In units 5–7 only 18–45% of the optional exercises were opened by anyone, against 97–100% in units 2–4 (78% in unit 1), so in the later units the class seems to have worked from a smaller selection.
  • Struggle rarely meant quitting. In the logged units, 262 attempts involved real struggle (2+ failed submissions or 3+ runs with errors). 90% of them still ended solved, against 94% of attempts without struggle.
  • Difficulty drives giving up. Students left 13% of engaged 1- and 2-point exercises unsolved, 30% of 3-point exercises and 4 of 6 challenge exercises.
  • Profiles. Quick solvers are at or below classmates' time and leave under 20% unsolved. Persisters are slower but still finish, and three of the seven are 1-point focused. Quick or moves on solve fast when they solve but leave a fifth or more. Struggles and moves on are slower and leave a fifth or more.
  • The profiles use all exercises, checkpoints included. Counting only the optional exercises gives almost the same give-up measure (ρ = 0.98), but students close to the cut-offs can switch profile.

07Creative-task code

Programs grew in both length and decisions over the course. For the 18 students with both a Creative Task 3 program and an individual final project, median length went from 51 to 126 lines and decisions (if/elif, for/while, and/or) from 8 to 34. Loops arrive late: 27% of Creative Task 3 programs use a for loop, against 86% of final projects.

  • "Creative Task 3" is the task handed in 3–11 February. Students used the creative-task slot of the unit they were in: 3.5.2 (12), 4.5 (7) or 2.5 (3).
  • Decisions track the teacher's grade better than length does: Creative Task 3 decisions vs its grade ρ = 0.55 (lines 0.41); final-project decisions vs final grade ρ = 0.74 (lines 0.57, number of course units' concepts used 0.56, minutes 0.44).
  • No copying between students: the largest 5-token overlap between programs by different students is 0.19, between two 4-line stubs. One program was a pasted course example and is excluded.
  • Two final projects are several times longer than the rest. All correlations are rank-based, so they don't drive them.

08Lesson pages

A student spent a median of about 3 minutes on a lesson page (3.4 in unit 2, 2.9 in unit 3, 2.6 in unit 4; unit 1 pages have no questions and got about a minute). In 23% of student–page pairs the student came back on a later day. First answers to the multiple-choice questions were right 66% of the time.

  • A habit, not a moment. Within the same student, quick and slow answers were equally often right (odds ratio 1.02 per doubling of reading time, 95% CI 0.81–1.27). Between students, those who generally read longer were right on the first try more often (odds ratio 1.79 per doubling, p < .001), and students who answered within 10 s more often got fewer first answers right (ρ = −0.55).
  • The lock-out. After the 2nd wrong answer (and again after the 4th and 6th) the next answer never came sooner than 10 s, with a median of 15 s. After the 1st, 3rd or 5th wrong answer students tried again within 2–10 s. So in this log the timeout follows every second wrong answer and lasts about 10 s, not 15 s after the third.
  • A few students hit the lock-out much more often than the rest (class median 3); most of them combine quick guessing with low first-try accuracy.
  • First-try accuracy is not clearly related to the final grade (ρ = 0.40; the interval includes zero). Coming back to lesson pages goes with lower grades only weakly (ρ = −0.29; the interval includes zero).
  • Reading time counts active time on the page, excluding time in other windows. Gaps up to 5 minutes count as reading, because reading produces no events.

09Distraction: window focus and blur

Four students (s1, s2, s3 and s4) were seen playing video games during lessons. Raw blur and focus counts don't single them out. Time away from CS Academy during lessons does: in the lessons they attended they were away for 47% of the time, against 25% for their classmates.

  • Why blur counts mislead. 36% of blur→focus pairs happen within one second and land on another CS Academy page, mostly switching between an exercise and the docs. The four had 23 blur events per lesson hour against 19 for the others (p = .77).
  • What does differ. Absences of a minute or more: 5.0 against 2.7 per lesson hour (p = .003). Lesson minutes with any activity: 40% against 56% (p = .02). They were away more in every one of the 11 lessons, most clearly in November and late January. In time away they rank 1st, 3rd, 5th and 6th of the 27.
  • What else they share. They worked mostly on 1-point exercises or did very few exercises, were behind or at the median in pace, and three of them hit the question lock-out far more often than most.
  • Away does not always mean gaming. It also covers listening to the teacher, using a desktop editor (the class opened the desktop CMU Graphics docs on 25 November) or reading elsewhere.

10Grades and self-efficacy

The behaviour measures line up with the grades in a consistent way: what students built and how efficiently they worked mattered more than how long they spent or how often they switched windows.

  • Strongest: final-project decisions (ρ = 0.74), minutes per point (−0.59), final-project concept breadth (0.56) and time against classmates on the same exercise (−0.52). Time away (−0.39) and long absences (−0.46) are negative too, but their intervals include zero.
  • Not clearly related: raw blur events (−0.09), first-try accuracy on lesson questions (0.40), lessons attended in the log period (−0.32) and failed submissions per solve (−0.13).
  • Pace groups (E = 1 … A = 5): usually ahead averages 4.62 and usually behind 3.00 (fewer than five graded students around the median); Kruskal–Wallis p = .101.
  • With 18 students the intervals are about ±0.4 wide, so read these as an ordering rather than precise effect sizes.

Self-efficacy before and after the course

21 of the 27 students could be linked to their survey answers. 18 answered the June 2025 survey (before the course) and 23 the August 2026 survey (after it); 17 answered both. For those 17 the mean rose from 4.30 to 5.08 on the 7-point scale (Wilcoxon p = .002), and 14 of 17 went up.

  • The June value already predicted the grade. Self-efficacy five months before the course correlates ρ = 0.73 with the final grade. Students who started confident were also more efficient (minutes per point ρ = −0.49), but they did not do more exercises (−0.01) or work further ahead (0.14).
  • Mastery and growth. Students who earned more exercise points (ρ = 0.36) or worked further ahead (0.30) gained a little more, but neither interval excludes zero. Holding the June value constant, the share of quick solves does not clearly add to the August value (p = .08). This would fit Bandura's mastery experiences as the main source of self-efficacy, but with 17 students the evidence is weak.
  • After the course it tracks performance closely: ρ = 0.80 with the final grade, 0.56 with the Creative Task 3 grade, 0.59 with the decisions in the Creative Task 3 program and −0.63 with minutes per point. The August survey came after the grades were out, so the grade itself is part of what students rated (holding June constant, adding the final grade raises R² from 0.69 to 0.89).
  • Pace. Median gains: usually ahead +1.0, around the median +0.3 (p = .136); fewer than five students in the other pace group answered both surveys.
  • Persistence. The two coping items, "trace correctly after earlier failures" and "write working code after hours of bugs", go with finishing and quick solving in August (failures item vs exercises left unsolved ρ = −0.47, vs quick solves 0.56), but not with how often students pushed through a logged struggle. The slower-but-finishing persisters rate themselves low (median 3.8 in August), so finishing despite difficulty did not turn into feeling capable.
  • AI self-efficacy (five new items in August) correlates 0.67 with the final grade, mostly through general self-efficacy (0.21 when August self-efficacy is held constant).
  • Largest gains: designing tests (+1.3), finding a bug given a design (+1.06), writing and calling functions (+1.06), tracing nested structures (+0.94) and tracing function calls (+0.94). Smallest: tracing with the same variable names (+0.2) and decomposing a problem (+0.5).

11Groups and trajectories

Not shown in this version. The six behaviour groups of the earlier version were chosen because Ward clustering with four groups agreed with k-means (adjusted Rand 0.80). Without the students who declined the use of their data that agreement is 0.34, and most groups would have fewer than five students, so the groups are left out until they are redone.

12Method notes

  • Files. The platform's grades-and-time export holds scores, completion times and minutes on task; two further exports repeat the same scores and were used only to name exercise sets. Point values come from the course's exercise list and match every observed score.
  • Exercise types. Checkpoints are the only mandatory exercises; all other exercises are optional (confirmed by the teacher).
  • Item status. Solved = full points; attempted = score 0 (submitted, never passed); opened = time on task but no score. Lesson pages have a constant score of 2 and count as done when they have a completion time.
  • Pace. 21 class days (10+ students completing something). Usually ahead or behind = above or below the class median on at least two thirds of the days from 13 January.
  • Errors. Same definitions as the TE22/TE23 error study: a spell follows one exact message on one page until a run without it; fresh = started by the student's own run of code they had edited; learning slope = log fix time on log2 of the k-th encounter with student fixed effects and student-clustered standard errors.
  • Lessons in the log. Minutes where at least a third of the class was active within ±4 minutes, with a peak of two thirds; 11 lessons between 18 November and 28 January.
  • Code. Static analysis of the saved programs (parsed, never run). Decisions = if + elif + for + while + and/or operators. Programs under 10 lines or mostly copied from an example are excluded.
  • Self-efficacy. The class's survey code list links answers to students. A survey counts when at least 10 of the 15 items were answered. Mean of the 15 common items; the five AI items in August are kept separate.
  • Privacy. The page shows the class and groups of students only. Grades and self-efficacy are summarised only for groups of five or more; the four students observed playing games are s1–s4, with their time-away measures only. The consent answers of the June 2025 and May 2026 surveys apply: five students who declined CS Academy data are left out entirely, eight students' grades are not used, and survey answers count only with a “Ja”.
  • Small numbers. 18 graded students: every correlation has a wide interval. The log-based parts cover units 1–4 only.
