Block, text, grades & self-efficacy

Exploratory overview of the TE22 and TE23 cohorts at NTI Uppsala

Built from organized/ · students identified by CS Academy user id (names are in code_book.csv) · exploratory, not confirmatory

What stands out

  1. The answer key can be recovered from the forms. One key reproduces every student's total score exactly in all three administrations, so per-question correctness is reliable without a separate key.
  2. Block is slightly easier than text, and the gap is in the second half of each section. Text matches or beats block on questions 3–8 but falls 14–40 points behind on every question from 9 to 14 (the loop questions, if the ordering in your description holds).
  3. Programming in free time matters most, and matters more for text. Each step up in free-time hours is worth ≈ points out of 28, about twice the effect of hours in compulsory school. Whether school was mostly block or mostly text shows no relationship with either score.
  4. On average, the chain you expected holds: experience → entry score → grade → self-efficacy, with ρ ≈ 0.5–0.6 at each link. The direct links that skip a step are weaker (ρ ≈ 0.3), which looks like a sequence rather than one underlying trait.
  5. About a third of students change lanes. Of students with both an entry score and a final grade, climbed (bottom half at entry → C or above) and slipped (top half at entry → D or below).
  6. Climbers and slippers differ in what they do, not what they knew. Climbers had little prior experience, but they handed everything in, worked outside class as much as the strongest group, and report self-efficacy close to the strongest group. Slippers had more to start with but more missing work and lower self-efficacy.
  7. Self-efficacy follows the grade more closely than the starting point (ρ vs ). Asking AI for help is unrelated to grades and to self-efficacy, so it is not a marker of struggling.
  8. The goal, help-seeking and habit items group by motivation, not by survey section (section 7). A stable two-factor structure (Engagement and Help from others) refines into four factors. Enjoyment and self-reliance is the factor most tied to grades, and climbers combine it with the most help-seeking from others.

Can the right answers be read from the survey?

Yes. Google Forms exports each student's total Score but not which questions were right. With 150 + 83 + 27 students there is only one answer key that reproduces every total, and searching for it gives the same key in all three forms, with zero mismatches. The two example questions are not scored, which leaves 28 scored questions: 14 block and 14 text.

Please check: your description adds up to 12 per section (2 assignment, 4 conditionals, 2 for, 2 while, 2 loop+conditional), but each section has 14. One reading that fits 14 is assignment 1–2, conditionals 3–8, for 9–10, while 11–12, loop+conditional 13–14. Under your 12-question description, positions 13–14 would be unlabelled. The overall findings don't depend on this mapping, but the per-concept conclusions do. If you tell me the order, I'll add concept labels.

A naive "most common answer" key would be wrong on question 26 (answer 6, most beginners say 4), question 29 (answer 1, most say 0) and, in 2023, question 15. The reconstructed key with % correct per question is in analysis/answer_key.csv.

Text questions 3–8 are answered as well as block, sometimes better: position 5 is 85% in text vs 52% in block. Text questions 9–14 are where students struggle (24–47%). Question 2 is the one early exception, where text trails block by 19 points. Question 13 is the hardest in both sections and still the hardest for TE22 two years later (68% block, 44% text). This pattern fits the literature on loop semantics in text (range bounds, update of the loop variable) being harder than block repeat. The block section also has a ceiling problem: score 13–14, which compresses differences at the top.

2 · Prior experience and the entry scores

Rank correlations with the entry scores

Three things are worth noting. First, free-time programming is the strongest predictor, and more so for text (ρ ≈ .45) than block (ρ ≈ .37), in both cohorts. Students who code on their own seem to bring the text-specific knowledge the block items don't need. Second, having done block programming at all (Scratch etc.) goes with higher scores in both sections, while having done text in school adds little. Third, whether school was mostly block or mostly text makes no difference, so the amount of exposure matters more than the modality. The few 15+ hour students dip slightly on block (n = 14), which looks like noise or ceiling effects.

4 · Grade pathways

TE23 grades mostly hold or rise. From the February estimate to the final grade, students went up, stayed the same and went down. The February estimate is already a strong predictor (ρ ). The May and June 2025 estimates are identical, so all later movement happened between June 2025 and the final grade, and it lines up with the final project (ρ project–final ). The final project is how students climb: three of the four two-step climbers got an A on it. TE22 is noisier (first unit → final ρ ): many students with an E on unit 4 ended anywhere from F to A.

What to look out for in future cohorts

  • Missing or ungraded units ("na") early in the course are the strongest warning sign. Two or more missing parts almost always ends in E/F.
  • An E or F estimate in February is not final, but recovery runs through the final project. Students who are not producing project work by May are the ones who stay low.
  • High entry score plus missing work is the "slipped" pattern (section 6). These students are easy to miss because they look strong on paper.
  • Low self-efficacy despite good grades (section 5) may matter for who chooses to continue with programming.
  • Data quality: in te22a, students have a final grade above every recorded component (e.g. F, F, F → B). These could be completions after the export or entry errors, and they should be checked before any te22a grade analysis.

5 · Self-efficacy and the SCT survey (May–June 2025)

n = students who consented. Items are scored 1–7. Subscales: tracing program flow (6 items), persistence/debugging/problem solving (6), control flow & functions (3), classes & recursion (5, not taught to everyone). Core = the first three together. All subscales have Cronbach's α ≥ .90.

Students are confident tracing code and writing conditionals, and much less confident about persisting and debugging when things go wrong. Persistence is the lowest of the core subscales in every class. te22b is lowest across the board, which matches its grades (more E grades than any other class). For help-seeking, trying it themselves comes first, then AI and classmates about equally, and the teacher last. AI help-seeking does not go with lower self-efficacy (ρ ) or lower grades (ρ ), so in this data heavy AI use is not a sign of struggling.

Story thread · Stalls

The logs show why persistence ranks lowest. Errors about meaning do not get faster with practice, and trial-and-error debugging does not decline with experience (Chapter 5: escape test, whack-a-mole). Getting unstuck is where mastery experiences are hardest to come by, and the survey reflects it.

6 · How the pieces fit together

Your basic model holds on average, and each link is roughly as strong as the next. But the links that skip a step are clearly weaker: free-time experience → grade is only ρ . That points to a chain of stages, where each stage opens a new chance to diverge, rather than one stable "programming ability" running through everything. The self-efficacy link is also cross-sectional: the SCT was collected near the end of the course, so it more likely reflects the grade (mastery experience, in Bandura's terms) than causes it.

Four profiles

Start = above/below the cohort median on the entry assessment. Finish = final grade C or above vs D or below. SCT measures only cover students who took the SCT (n shown).

Climbers start with as little free-time experience as the struggling group and below-median block and text scores. What sets them apart is behavior: almost no missing work, as much work outside class as the steady-strong group, and self-efficacy nearly as high. They are the clearest example of students working their way up.

Slipped students started above the median (block 11.2/14) but have the most missing work, the least work outside class among the higher starters, and self-efficacy as low as the struggling group. These are the students who "fall off the train". In TE22 they outnumber climbers 10 to 6.

Steady struggling students are the most "just pass the course" motivated (highest pass courses, lowest fun) and use AI help the most. Steady strong students have the highest prior experience and enjoy programming the most.

Story thread · Improves and drops

What separates climbers from slippers is visible in the logs too. Finishing the exercises you attempt is the strongest log correlate of self-efficacy (ρ .41, Chapter 2), and in te22a after-school minutes track the final-project grade (ρ .64, Chapter 6). Missing work is the grade-book side of missed weeks: after an absent week, the next week is absent 41% of the time (Chapter 3).

So is behaviour static?

Mostly, with important exceptions. Grades within a course are sticky (the Feb estimate predicts the final grade at ρ ≈ .8), and entry score predicts the final grade at ρ ≈ .5. Still, about 3 in 10 students end up somewhere their starting point wouldn't predict, and the variables that separate them (handing work in, working outside class, enjoyment, self-efficacy) are things a teacher can actually influence. Prior experience is not. For a paper, the profile split is a good motivation for a person-centred analysis (latent profile or latent transition analysis) once more cohorts are added. With about 100 students, the four groups here should be read as descriptive.

Future & utility: learning to prepare for university, get a job, make projects and understand the digital world, plus doing schoolwork outside class.

Enjoyment & self-reliance: learning because it's fun, trying to solve problems yourself, finishing tasks in class, and own projects in free time.

In the two-factor view the first two merge into one Engagement factor, and the course-bound items drop out.

What this adds to the profiles: enjoyment and self-reliance is the scale most tied to grades (ρ ) and self-efficacy (ρ ). Help from others is unrelated to grades, but it is highest among climbers and lowest among students who slipped. Climbers match the steady-strong group on enjoyment and future goals and ask for help more. Slippers are as low on future goals as the struggling group, in between on enjoyment, and the least likely of all four groups to ask for help. Steady-struggling students are the most course-bound and enjoy programming least. With 11–12 students in each small group these differences are suggestive, but they fit the climber story: motivated students who also ask for help move up. TE22 and TE23 do not differ significantly on any scale.

Caveats

  • Consent: rows where students answered "Nej" are excluded (). Grades for students who never took the SCT have unknown StudyBee consent and are included here; check this against your ethics approval.
  • Missing identities: 23 TE22 students have no name or email in the source files, so their assessment answers can't be linked to grades or the SCT.
  • Small groups: the class-level and profile-level numbers rest on 10–50 students each. Treat differences as hypotheses.
  • Grades are on a 0–5 scale (F = 0 … A = 5) for correlations; te22 and te23 grading structures differ (units vs repeated overall estimates).
  • Timing: the SCT (2025) was collected after or near the end of the course, so it can't be read as a predictor of those grades.
  • Factor analysis: about 90 students for 16 items is at the low end for EFA. Factors with 3 items have modest reliability, and the structure was found and scored in the same sample, so it should be confirmed with a CFA on a new cohort.
