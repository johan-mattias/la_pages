Where students start and finish

Block and text entry scores, grades and self-efficacy in the TE22 and TE23 cohorts at NTI Uppsala: an exploratory overview

the page shows groups and anonymous points, never single students · exploratory, not confirmatory

What stands out

  1. The answer key can be recovered from the forms. One key reproduces every student's total score exactly in all three administrations, so per-question correctness is reliable without a separate key.
  2. Block is slightly easier than text, and the gap is in the second half of each section. Text matches or beats block on questions 3–8 but falls 14–40 points behind on every question from 9 to 14 (most likely the loop questions).
  3. Programming in free time matters most, and matters more for text. Each step up in free-time hours is worth ≈ points out of 28, about twice the effect of hours in compulsory school. Whether school was mostly block or mostly text shows no relationship with either score.
  4. On average, the expected chain holds: experience → entry score → grade → self-efficacy, with ρ ≈ 0.4–0.55 at each link. The direct links that skip a step are weaker (ρ ≈ 0.2–0.35), which looks like a sequence rather than one underlying trait.
  5. About a third of students change lanes. Of students with both an entry score and a final grade, climbed (bottom half at entry → C or above) and slipped (top half at entry → D or below).
  6. Climbers and slippers differ in what they do, not what they knew. Climbers had little prior experience, but they handed everything in, worked outside class as much as the strongest group, and report higher self-efficacy than either group that finished low. Slippers had more to start with but more missing work and the lowest self-efficacy of the four groups.
  7. Self-efficacy follows the grade more closely than the starting point (ρ vs ). Asking AI for help is unrelated to grades and to self-efficacy, so it is not a marker of struggling.
  8. The goal, help-seeking and habit items group by motivation, not by survey section (section 7). A stable two-factor structure (Engagement and Help from others) refines into four factors. Enjoyment and self-reliance is the factor most tied to grades, and climbers combine it with the most help-seeking from others.

2 · Prior experience and the entry scores

Programming in free time is the strongest predictor of the entry scores, and more so for text (ρ ≈ .45) than for block (ρ ≈ .37), in both cohorts. In a regression with both, each step of free-time hours adds ≈ points (of 28) and each step of school hours ≈. Whether school was mostly block or mostly text makes no difference, so the amount of exposure matters more than the modality. Chapter 12 has the analysis for the study classes.

3 · TE22 two years later (same test, Jan 2025)

Everyone improves, and most reach the ceiling. The mean rose from to (of 28), and of students now score 26–28, so the test can no longer tell the stronger third-year students apart. Measuring growth past year 1 would need harder items (nested loops, functions, lists). Chapter 12 has the follow-up for the study classes.

4 · Grade pathways

TE23 grades mostly hold or rise. From the February estimate to the final grade, students went up, stayed the same and went down. The February estimate is already a strong predictor (ρ ). The May and June 2025 estimates are identical, so all later movement happened between June 2025 and the final grade, and it lines up with the final project (ρ project–final ). The final project is how students climb: five of the eight students who rose two or more steps got an A on it (for te23c this partly holds by definition, since its final grade is the final-project grade). TE22 is noisier (first unit → final ρ ): many students with an E on unit 4 ended anywhere from F to A.

What to look out for in future cohorts

  • Missing or ungraded units ("na") early in the course are the strongest warning sign. Two or more missing parts almost always ends in E/F.
  • An E or F estimate in February is not final, but recovery runs through the final project. Students who are not producing project work by May are the ones who stay low.
  • High entry score plus missing work is the "slipped" pattern (section 6). These students are easy to miss because they look strong on paper.
  • Low self-efficacy despite good grades (section 5) may matter for who chooses to continue with programming.
  • Data quality: in te22a, students have a final grade above every recorded component (e.g. F, F, F → B). These could be completions after the export or entry errors, and they should be checked before any te22a grade analysis.

5 · Self-efficacy and the SCT survey (May–June 2025)

n = students who consented. Items are scored 1–7. Subscales: tracing program flow (6 items), persistence/debugging/problem solving (6), control flow & functions (3), classes & recursion (5, not taught to everyone). Core = the first three together. All subscales have Cronbach's α ≥ .90.

Students are confident tracing code and writing conditionals, and much less confident about persisting and debugging when things go wrong. Persistence is the lowest of the core subscales in every class. te22b is lowest across the board, which matches its grades (more E grades than any other class). For help-seeking, trying it themselves comes first, then AI and classmates about equally, and the teacher last. AI help-seeking does not go with lower self-efficacy (ρ ) or lower grades (ρ ), so in this data heavy AI use is not a sign of struggling.

Story thread · Stalls

The logs show why persistence ranks lowest. Errors about meaning do not get faster with practice, and trial-and-error debugging does not decline with experience (Chapter 6: escape test, whack-a-mole). Getting unstuck is where mastery experiences are hardest to come by, and the survey reflects it.

"Who improved most in self-efficacy?" — not answerable yet

The SCT was given once (2025), so there is no individual change to measure. The earlier forms used different scales: attitudes in 2022, and a general self-efficacy scale in 2023/2025 whose answer options changed between rounds. A later SCT round would give these students a true second wave; Chapter 9 follows one class, te24c, surveyed before and after its course.

The closest thing available now is calibration: self-efficacy compared with the grade the student actually received (ranks within cohort). Students fall on both sides: some rate themselves well above their grade, and some capable students rate themselves well below it. The second kind may matter most for who continues with programming.

Story thread · Improvement, defined

Until a second wave exists, the story reads improvement through its main source instead: the mastery experiences recorded in the logs, such as finishing what you attempt and getting past errors (Chapter 2), and the grades that self-efficacy tracks. See how the story defines improvement.

6 · How the pieces fit together

The expected chain holds on average, and each link is roughly as strong as the next. But the links that skip a step are clearly weaker: free-time experience → grade is only ρ . That points to a chain of stages, where each stage opens a new chance to diverge, rather than one stable "programming ability" running through everything. The self-efficacy link is also cross-sectional: the SCT was collected near the end of the course, so it more likely reflects the grade (mastery experience, in Bandura's terms) than causes it.

Four profiles

Start = above/below the cohort median on the entry assessment. Finish = final grade C or above vs D or below. SCT measures only cover students who took the SCT (n shown).

Climbers start with even less free-time experience than the struggling group and below-median block and text scores. What sets them apart is behavior: almost no missing work, as much work outside class as the steady-strong group, and self-efficacy between the steady-strong and the struggling groups. They are the clearest example of students working their way up.

Slipped students started above the median (block /14) but have the most missing work, the least work outside class among the higher starters, and the lowest self-efficacy of the four groups. These are the students who "fall off the train". In TE22 they outnumber climbers to .

Steady struggling students are the most "just pass the course" motivated (highest pass courses, lowest fun). How often students ask AI for help barely differs between the four groups. Steady strong students have the highest prior experience and enjoy programming the most.

Story thread · Improves and drops

What separates climbers from slippers is visible in the logs too. Finishing the exercises you attempt is the strongest log correlate of self-efficacy (ρ .46, Chapter 2), and in te22a after-school minutes track the final-project grade (ρ .64, Chapter 7). Missing work is the grade-book side of missed weeks: after an absent week, the next week is absent 41% of the time (Chapter 3).

So is behaviour static?

Mostly, with important exceptions. Grades within a course are sticky (the Feb estimate predicts the final grade at ρ ), and entry score predicts the final grade at ρ . Still, about 3 in 10 students end up somewhere their starting point wouldn't predict, and the variables that separate them (handing work in, working outside class, enjoyment, self-efficacy) are things a teacher can actually influence. Prior experience is not. The profile split motivates a person-centred analysis (latent profile or latent transition analysis) once more cohorts are added. With students, the four groups here are descriptive, and Chapter 5 finds that the log data form one continuous population, so read them as regions of it rather than as types.

7 · Goals, help-seeking and habits: what goes together?

Exploratory factor analysis of the 16 items at the end of the SCT survey: 8 goal items, 4 help-seeking items and 4 habit items, with TE22 and TE23 pooled (n = complete answers out of ). The emotion items are left out because only 4 students answered them.

Two factors are very stable, and four fit acceptably and split the first into interpretable parts; the survey sections themselves are too unreliable to sum. Chapter 12 has the factor analysis for the consented study classes, with model fit and loadings. The four factors:

Future & utility: learning to prepare for university, get a job, make projects and understand the digital world, plus doing schoolwork outside class.

Enjoyment & self-reliance: learning because it's fun, trying to solve problems yourself, finishing tasks in class, and own projects in free time.

Help from others: asking classmates, the teacher and AI.

Course-bound / no goal: no particular goal, learning mainly to pass, and working outside school only before deadlines.

In the two-factor view the first two merge into one Engagement factor, and the course-bound items drop out.

What this adds to the profiles: enjoyment and self-reliance is the scale most tied to grades (ρ ) and self-efficacy (ρ ). Help from others is unrelated to grades, but it is highest among climbers; the other three groups are about the same. Climbers are close to the steady-strong group on enjoyment and future goals and ask for help more. Slippers are as low on future goals as the struggling group and in between on enjoyment. Climbers and steady-struggling students are the most course-bound, and steady-struggling students enjoy programming least. With 11–12 students in each small group these differences are suggestive, but they fit the climber story: motivated students who also ask for help move up. TE22 and TE23 do not differ significantly on any scale.

Caveats

  • Consent: assessment and survey answers are used only with a “Ja” to their own consent question, and grades are left out for students who said “Nej” to StudyBee data (). Grades of students who never answered the survey, and so never answered the consent question, are included, as in the other chapters.
  • Missing identities: 6 TE22 students can't be linked to assessment answers: 5 don't appear in any TE22 form, and 1 is missing from the login lists. One link was made from a near-identical spelling.
  • Small groups: the class-level and profile-level numbers rest on 10–50 students each. Treat differences as hypotheses.
  • Grades are on a 0–5 scale (F = 0 … A = 5) for correlations; te22 and te23 grading structures differ (units vs repeated overall estimates).
  • Timing: the SCT (2025) was collected after or near the end of the course, so it can't be read as a predictor of those grades.
  • Factor analysis: about 90 students for 16 items is at the low end for EFA. Factors with 3 items have modest reliability, and the structure was found and scored in the same sample, so it should be confirmed with a CFA on a new cohort.
